Technical Specifications & Performance

This section defines 30 key technical performance terms for roofing: from structural loads and minimum pitch to thermal insulation values, fire ratings, acoustic performance, wind resistance and impact testing. These parameters govern the specification and independent certification of Metrotile lightweight steel roofing tiles under BBA Certificate 07/4470 and relevant British and European standards.

Pitch & Geometry

Minimum Pitch
Minimum pitch is the lowest slope angle at which a given roofing tile or profile can be installed while maintaining weathertightness. Below the minimum pitch, the overlap between tile courses is insufficient to prevent wind-driven rain from travelling uphill beneath the tiles. For traditional clay and concrete tiles, the minimum pitch is typically 17.5–22°. Most Metrotile steel tile profiles can be installed from 10°, with Aquapan and Cambridge Xtreme 9.5 Shingle specified for pitches as low as 9.5°–10°. Minimum pitch values are confirmed in the BBA Certificate 07/4470 for each profile and exposure zone.

See also: Exposure zone · Aquapan

Pitch Angle
Pitch angle is the angle of the roof slope measured in degrees from the horizontal plane. A pitch of 0° is flat; 90° is vertical. Common domestic pitches in the UK are 30–45°. Low-pitch roofs are generally 10–17.5°. The pitch angle is critical for tile specification (it determines the minimum overlap required) and for structural calculations (it determines snow and wind loads). It can be measured with an inclinometer, spirit level and rule, or calculated from the rise and half-span.

See also: Pitch angle (roof types)

Low Pitch
Low pitch (in UK roofing practice, typically below 17.5°) is defined by the lower limit of traditional tile applicability. Standard clay and concrete interlocking tiles cannot be installed below approximately 17.5–22° without unacceptable risk of wind-driven rain ingress. Steel tile profiles that are engineered for low pitch — such as Aquapan and Cambridge Xtreme — achieve lower minimum pitches by increasing the tile-to-tile lap and adding secondary sealing features. Low-pitch applications require more conservative batten gauges (tighter spacing) to maintain adequate lap.
Exposure Zone
Exposure zones classify the UK into regions of similar wind-driven rain severity, used to determine the required tile fixing specification and minimum pitch. BS 5534 and the BBA certificate define four exposure categories: Sheltered, Moderate, Severe and Very Severe. Zone boundaries follow the UK Met Office wind and rainfall data. Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland, south-west England and all coastal strips generally fall in Severe or Very Severe zones. The correct exposure zone must be identified for every Metrotile installation to ensure the right number and type of fixings are specified.

See also: Exposure category · BS 5534

Structural Loads

Dead Load
Dead load is the permanent, static self-weight of the roof structure and covering — the weight of the tiles, battens, underlay, structural timbers and any ceiling or insulation. Dead load is expressed in kN/m² or kg/m² of plan area. Clay tiles typically impose a dead load of 40–55 kg/m²; concrete interlocking tiles 40–50 kg/m²; natural slate 30–40 kg/m². Metrotile steel tiles impose approximately 7–8 kg/m² of dead load from the tiles alone (plus battens, underlay and structure), making them the lowest-dead-load tiled roofing option and the preferred choice for re-roofing projects where the existing structure has limited spare capacity.

See also: Live load · Imposed load

Live Load
Live load (also called imposed load in UK practice) is a variable load that acts on the roof due to temporary causes — primarily snow, maintenance workers, and wind. In structural calculations, live load is combined with dead load to give the total design load. Roof live loads are specified in BS EN 1991-1-1 (EuroCode 1, Part 1-1: densities, self-weight, imposed loads) and include 0.6 kN/m² for maintenance access on pitched roofs and snow loads determined by altitude and geographic location.

See also: Dead load · Snow load

Snow Load
Snow load is the imposed load on a roof due to accumulated snow, expressed in kN/m² on plan. UK snow loads are determined from the snow map in BS EN 1991-1-3 (EuroCode 1, Part 1-3) and depend on altitude and geographic location. At sea level in most of England and Wales, the characteristic snow load is approximately 0.5–0.6 kN/m²; in Scotland and at high altitude, it can reach 1.5–2.5 kN/m² or more. Snow load reduces with increasing pitch angle (snow slides off steep roofs). The lightweight Metrotile system produces a low combined dead + snow load on the structure compared to heavier tile alternatives.
Wind Uplift
Wind uplift is the suction force that wind creates on the upper surface of a roof as air flows over it, tending to lift the tiles and roof covering away from the structure. It is most severe at roof edges (eaves, ridges, hips, verges) and at corner zones. Wind uplift is a function of wind speed, pitch angle and the building's local topography. BS 5534 and EuroCode 1 (BS EN 1991-1-4) provide the design methods. Metrotile tiles are mechanically nailed to battens with annular ring shank nails, providing high resistance to wind uplift that is verified in the BBA certificate.

See also: Wind load · Fixing specification

Wind Load
Wind load encompasses both the positive pressure on windward faces and the suction (negative pressure) on leeward faces and roof surfaces. For roofing tiles, the critical component is wind uplift (suction). Wind loads are calculated from the basic wind velocity at the site (derived from the UK wind speed map in BS EN 1991-1-4), the building height, terrain category, orography factor and the pressure coefficients for the roof geometry. The resulting design wind uplift load is compared with the mechanical resistance of the tile fixing system.
Imposed Load
Imposed load is the UK/European structural engineering term equivalent to live load — any variable load from occupancy, maintenance, snow or wind. For roof structural calculations, the imposed load includes snow (from BS EN 1991-1-3) and a 0.6 kN/m² maintenance load (uniformly distributed, or 1.0 kN concentrated load for access). Imposed loads are combined with dead loads using partial safety factors to give the design ultimate limit state loads for structural member sizing.

Thermal Performance

U-Value
U-value (thermal transmittance) is the rate of heat transfer through a building element per unit area per degree of temperature difference between inside and outside (W/m²K). The lower the U-value, the better the insulation. Building Regulations Part L sets maximum U-values for new and replacement roofs: 0.18 W/m²K for new roofs in new dwellings; 0.16 W/m²K for reroofing of existing dwellings. The steel tiles themselves contribute a U-value of approximately 50–100 W/m²K (essentially negligible resistance), so the insulation layer below dominates. Metrotile can supply U-value calculations for any specified insulation build-up.

See also: R-value · Building Regulations Part L

R-Value
R-value (thermal resistance) is the reciprocal of U-value (m²K/W) — it measures the resistance of a material or building element to heat flow rather than its transmittance. The R-value of a composite wall or roof is the sum of the R-values of each layer (tiles, battens, underlay, insulation, air gaps, plasterboard). Higher R-values indicate better insulation. A 150 mm PIR rigid foam board (λ = 0.022 W/mK) has an R-value of 6.82 m²K/W — typically the largest contributor to the total roof R-value.
Thermal Mass
Thermal mass is the ability of a material to absorb, store and release heat over time. High thermal mass materials (concrete, brick, stone) moderate temperature swings by absorbing heat during the day and releasing it at night. Lightweight steel tiles have negligible thermal mass — they heat up and cool down rapidly with outside temperature. This means that the internal temperature of the roof space is governed primarily by the insulation layer, not by the tile itself. In PassivHaus and low-energy buildings, high thermal mass is often desired elsewhere in the structure; the lightweight roof system is not a disadvantage as long as insulation is adequate.

See also: PassivHaus

Thermal Bridge
A thermal bridge is a localised area of significantly higher heat transfer through a building element — typically at a structural penetration (rafter, purlin, steel beam) through the insulation layer. In insulated pitched roofs, the rafters create linear thermal bridges through the mineral wool or PIR insulation between them. Thermal bridges increase the effective U-value of the roof above the calculated centre-of-cavity value. Metrotile systems with continuous rigid insulation above rafter level minimise thermal bridging compared to between-rafter insulation alone.
Dew Point
Dew point is the temperature at which water vapour in the air condenses into liquid water. In roof construction, the dew point is the critical temperature at which interstitial condensation can form within the roof build-up if warm moist air from inside the building reaches a cold surface. Condensation analysis (to BS 5250 and BS EN 13788) determines the position of the dew point within the roof cross-section for the design temperature and humidity conditions. Correct positioning of the vapour control layer (VCL) above the dew point prevents condensation from forming within the insulation or on the structural timbers.

See also: Condensation risk

Condensation Risk
Condensation risk is assessed for any insulated roof to confirm that moisture will not accumulate in the roof structure over time, leading to timber rot and insulation degradation. Two types are considered: surface condensation (on the underside of the roof deck or tiles) and interstitial condensation (within the build-up). BS 5250 and Approved Document C provide guidance. Metrotile's breathable underlay (vapour-permeable membrane) allows water vapour from the batten zone to diffuse outwards, reducing interstitial condensation risk compared to impermeable (Type 5) felts.

Fire Performance

Fire Rating
Fire rating is a classification of a material's or system's resistance to or behaviour in fire. For roofing materials, two distinct fire performance regimes are relevant: reaction to fire (how the material behaves when exposed to a fire source — does it ignite, spread flame or produce smoke?) and resistance to fire (how long a structural element or assembly prevents fire and smoke from passing through). Metrotile steel tiles are classified for reaction to fire (Class A) and for external fire exposure (BROOF rating).

See also: Reaction to fire · BROOF classification

External Fire Exposure
External fire exposure refers to the performance of the roof covering when exposed to fire from outside the building — typically from burning debris falling or blowing onto the roof from a neighbouring fire. Building Regulations Part B (Fire Safety) and Approved Document B classify roofs by their resistance to external fire exposure (BROOF, CROOF etc.) and restrict the use of certain roofing materials in proximity to boundaries or other buildings. Metrotile steel tiles achieve a BROOF(t4) classification under BS EN 13501-5, making them acceptable in all positions including proximity to site boundaries.
Reaction to Fire
Reaction to fire classifies how a material contributes to the growth of a fire — whether it ignites, spreads flame, produces heat or drips molten material. The European classification system (BS EN 13501-1) uses classes A1, A2, B, C, D, E and F from non-combustible to easily flammable. Metrotile steel tiles are classified as Class A1 (non-combustible) under BS EN 13501-1 for their reaction to fire. This is the highest possible classification and means the tiles will not ignite or contribute to fire spread under any realistic fire scenario.

See also: BROOF classification

BROOF(t4) Classification
BROOF(t4) is the European classification for the performance of a complete roof system (not just the tile) under external fire exposure testing (test method t4, as defined in BS EN 13501-5). A BROOF classification means the roof system has an acceptable performance against external fire — it will not provide undue spread of flame across the outer surface. Building Regulations Approved Document B requires roofs within 6 m of a site boundary to achieve a minimum BROOF(t4) designation (or equivalent national designation). Metrotile steel tiles achieve BROOF(t4), confirmed in BBA Certificate 07/4470, making them suitable for use at site boundaries.

Acoustic & Impact Performance

Acoustic Performance
Acoustic performance of a roof measures its ability to attenuate airborne sound (external noise from traffic, aircraft, weather) and to absorb impact sound (rain, hail). For roofing, the two most relevant parameters are the weighted sound reduction index (Rw) and the rain noise level. The stone chip surface of Metrotile tiles provides significantly better rain noise absorption than smooth metal sheets, and the insulated roof build-up (underlay, batten zone, insulation, plasterboard ceiling) provides the main structural sound reduction. Typical Rw values for a fully insulated Metrotile roof are in the range 40–50 dB.

See also: Rw value · Rain noise

Rw Value
Rw (weighted sound reduction index) is the single-number descriptor for the airborne sound insulation performance of a building element, measured in decibels (dB). It is determined by laboratory testing to BS EN ISO 10140 and weighted across a range of frequencies to give a single number representative of human hearing sensitivity. A higher Rw value indicates better acoustic insulation. Building Regulations Part E (Resistance to the Passage of Sound) sets minimum Rw requirements for separating floors and walls in residential buildings but does not specifically regulate roof Rw. However, specifiers in sensitive locations (near airports, motorways) may specify a minimum Rw for the roof assembly.
Rain Noise
Rain noise is the impact sound generated by rain drops striking the roof covering — one of the main complaints about metal roofing. Smooth profiled steel sheets and aluminium roofing are notorious for amplifying rain noise. Metrotile steel tiles significantly reduce this problem: the stone chip aggregate surface absorbs and disperses the impact energy of rain drops before it can excite the steel sheet, reducing the radiated sound level by approximately 10–15 dB compared to smooth steel sheets. The additional attenuation from the insulated ceiling bring total rain noise in a Metrotile-tiled room to similar or lower levels than under a traditional tiled roof.
Impact Resistance
Impact resistance is the ability of a roofing material to withstand the mechanical impact of falling objects such as hailstones, debris or tools dropped during maintenance without cracking, splitting or penetration. Steel is inherently more impact-resistant than fired clay or concrete — it will dent before cracking, and a dented steel tile remains weathertight. Metrotile tiles are tested for impact resistance to BS EN 14782 and to the UEHJ (Urban Hail test) protocol, achieving Class 3 (45 mm, 23.5 m/s impact velocity) or better.

See also: Hail resistance

Hail Resistance
Hail resistance classifies a roofing material's ability to withstand hailstone impact without cracking, splitting or losing waterproofing performance. The European classification is based on testing with steel balls of defined diameter dropped from defined heights (per BS EN ISO 4892 and UEHJ protocols). Metrotile steel tiles achieve Class 3 hail resistance (equivalent to hailstone diameter up to 45 mm) — far exceeding the performance of clay or concrete tiles, which can crack or shatter under severe hail impact. Hail resistance is particularly valued in Scotland, the north of England and elevated areas prone to severe convective storms.

Durability & Weathering

Corrosion Resistance
Corrosion resistance is the ability of a metal to resist deterioration by oxidation and chemical attack. For steel roofing, corrosion resistance is determined by the metallic coating system applied to the steel substrate. Metrotile uses ZAM® (Zinc-Aluminium-Magnesium) coating — typically 150–185 g/m² coating weight — which provides superior corrosion resistance to conventional hot-dip galvanising. ZAM® coating offers approximately three times the corrosion resistance of conventional galvanising in neutral salt spray testing, giving Metrotile steel tiles exceptional durability even in coastal and industrial environments.

See also: ZAM® coating

Salt Spray Test
Salt spray testing (neutral salt spray, NSS, to BS EN ISO 9227) is the standard accelerated corrosion test for coated metal products. A specimen is exposed to a continuous fog of 5% sodium chloride solution at 35°C, and the time to first visible corrosion (red rust) is recorded. ZAM®-coated steel used in Metrotile tiles achieves in excess of 3,000 hours to first red rust in neutral salt spray testing, compared to approximately 1,000 hours for conventional galvanised steel. This accelerated test result correlates to exceptional real-world durability in coastal environments.

See also: ZAM® coating · Corrosion resistance

UV Resistance
UV resistance is the ability of a surface coating or material to resist photodegradation — the bleaching, chalking and embrittlement caused by long-term exposure to ultraviolet radiation from sunlight. The stone chip surface of Metrotile tiles is bonded with an acrylic binder that is UV-stabilised and provides a degree of UV screening to the coating layers below. The stone chip aggregate itself is UV-stable (mineral). Colour stability of the stone chip surface is tested over the expected service life by accelerated UV weathering tests (BS EN ISO 4892).

See also: UV resistance (materials)

Kerb Flashing
Kerb flashings are upstand flashings that seal the junction between a flat or low-pitch roofing membrane and a vertical element — such as a parapet wall, plant room, rooflight curb, lift shaft or chimney. On buildings with a combination of flat and pitched roof zones, kerb flashings prevent water from the flat roof from tracking behind the pitched roof covering at the eaves. They are typically formed in lead, aluminium or stainless steel and must allow differential thermal and structural movement between the roof structure and the upstand.
Weathertightness
Weathertightness is the overall ability of the roof system to prevent the ingress of rainwater, wind-driven rain, snow and condensation into the building interior. For Metrotile, weathertightness is demonstrated by the BBA Certificate 07/4470, which confirms that the system — when installed in accordance with the installation guide — achieves the required weathertightness for all UK exposure zones from the specified minimum pitch. Weathertightness depends on the combination of tile design, lap, batten gauge, underlay specification, flashing details and fixing specification.

See also: BBA Certificate
