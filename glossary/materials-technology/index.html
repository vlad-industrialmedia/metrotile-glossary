This section defines the materials science and engineering terminology behind Metrotile steel roofing tiles — from the steel substrate and metallic protective coatings through to the stone chip surface finish and whole-tile weight properties. Understanding the material stack explains why Metrotile tiles deliver outstanding durability over a 40-year design life.

Steel Substrate

Cold-rolled steel
Cold-rolled steel is steel that has been processed at or near room temperature (below its recrystallisation temperature), as opposed to hot-rolled steel which is formed at high temperature. Cold rolling increases the material's yield strength and hardness through work hardening, and produces a smoother surface finish and tighter dimensional tolerances compared to hot rolling. Metrotile tile profiles are cold-rolled from pre-coated steel coil, allowing precise forming of complex interlocking profiles without cracking the metallic coating. Cold-rolling also improves the steel's suitability for paint and stone chip adhesion.
High-tensile steel
High-tensile steel (also called high-strength steel) refers to steel with a yield strength significantly above that of mild steel — typically ≥350 MPa for roofing applications. Metrotile uses high-tensile steel in its tile substrates to achieve an excellent strength-to-weight ratio: the tiles are thin enough to be lightweight (7–8 kg/m²) whilst retaining structural rigidity under wind uplift, foot traffic during installation and hail impact. The Cambridge Xtreme 9.5 profile uses heavier-gauge high-tensile steel for additional robustness in extreme exposure conditions.

See also: Steel gauge · Cambridge Xtreme 9.5

Steel gauge
Steel gauge refers to the thickness of the steel sheet, typically expressed in millimetres (mm) for European roofing products. Standard Metrotile profiles use steel substrates of approximately 0.4–0.55mm nominal thickness, with the Cambridge Xtreme 9.5 using a heavier gauge. A thicker (heavier) gauge steel provides greater rigidity and impact resistance but increases tile weight and cost. The "9.5" in Cambridge Xtreme 9.5 Shingle refers to its steel specification, distinguishing it from the lighter standard shingle.
Substrate
In roofing materials terminology, the substrate refers to the base material to which protective coatings are applied. For Metrotile tiles, the substrate is the cold-rolled steel sheet. The performance of the overall tile system depends on the interaction between the substrate and all coating layers: metallic alloy coating (ZAM® or ZINCALUME®), organic primer, acrylic adhesive and stone chip aggregate. A well-prepared steel substrate ensures good bonding of all layers and prevents delamination over the product's lifetime.
Press forming
Press forming (or stamp forming / pressing) is the manufacturing process by which flat pre-coated steel sheet is formed into a three-dimensional tile profile using a hydraulic or mechanical press and matched dies. The die set stamps the steel into the required profile shape — ribs, embossing, interlocking edges — in a single press stroke or a series of progressive die stages. The precision of the press tooling determines the consistency of tile dimensions, which is critical for interlocking fit, batten gauge accuracy and visual uniformity on the finished roof.

Synonyms: stamping · pressing · profiling

Metallic Protective Coatings

ZAM® coating
ZAM® is a proprietary zinc-aluminium-magnesium (Zn-Al-Mg) metallic alloy coating applied by continuous hot-dip process to the steel substrate of Metrotile roofing tiles. The ZAM alloy composition (approximately 89% Zn, 6% Al, 3% Mg) provides multifunctional corrosion protection combining: (1) barrier protection from the dense aluminium-zinc oxide layer; (2) cathodic (sacrificial) protection from the zinc and zinc-magnesium phases; and (3) a self-healing mechanism in which magnesium ions migrate to exposed areas (scratches, cut edges) and form a stable, protective oxide layer. In standardised salt spray testing, ZAM-coated steel achieves 10–20× the corrosion resistance of equivalent conventional hot-dip galvanising. ZAM® is a registered trademark of Nisshin Steel, Japan.

See also: ZnAlMg · Hot-dip galvanising · Cathodic protection

ZnAlMg — Zinc-Aluminium-Magnesium alloy
ZnAlMg is the general chemical designation for the class of zinc-aluminium-magnesium metallic coating alloys used in steel roofing and other construction products. These alloys were developed in the 1990s as a performance-superior alternative to conventional hot-dip galvanising. The three principal alloying elements work synergistically: zinc provides sacrificial cathodic protection; aluminium strengthens the oxide barrier layer and improves adhesion; magnesium refines the microstructure and promotes self-healing at defects. Multiple proprietary versions exist: ZAM® (Nisshin Steel), Magnelis® (ArcelorMittal), SuperDyma® (Nippon Steel), ZM310 (Tata Steel).
Hot-dip galvanising
Hot-dip galvanising is the process of applying a protective zinc coating to steel by immersing it in a bath of molten zinc at approximately 450°C. As the steel is withdrawn and cooled, a series of zinc-iron alloy layers form, topped by a pure zinc outer layer. Hot-dip galvanising provides excellent cathodic protection — the zinc corrodes sacrificially in preference to the steel — and is the most widely used corrosion protection method for steel construction products worldwide. Standard galvanising (to BS EN ISO 1461) provides useful protection for many decades in moderate environments, but ZnAlMg coatings outperform it significantly in aggressive or coastal environments.

Synonyms: hot dip zinc coating · GI (galvanised iron) · HDG

ZINCALUME®
ZINCALUME® is a zinc-aluminium alloy coating (55% Al, 43.5% Zn, 1.5% Si) applied by continuous hot-dip process to steel, providing superior barrier protection and high-temperature resistance compared to conventional galvanising. ZINCALUME® was developed by Lysaght/BlueScope Steel and is used as the substrate for the Decra range of premium steel roofing tiles supplied by Metrotile. The aluminium-rich alloy provides excellent barrier protection against corrosion, good adhesion for stone chip coatings, and resistance to UV degradation. ZINCALUME® is equivalent to Galvalume® (sold in the Americas and Europe) and AZ (AlZn) grades per BS EN 10346.

Synonyms: Galvalume · AZ150 coating · aluminium-zinc alloy coating

Galvalume
Galvalume is the generic (non-proprietary) term for the zinc-aluminium silicon alloy metallic coating equivalent to ZINCALUME®, widely used in North America and Europe. The standard composition is 55% aluminium, 43.5% zinc and 1.5% silicon. Galvalume offers approximately 2–4× the service life of conventional hot-dip galvanising in most atmospheric environments, and is specified under ASTM A792 (US) and BS EN 10346 grade AZ (Europe). In the UK roofing market, the term ZINCALUME® is more commonly used, but both terms refer to the same coating type.

See also: ZINCALUME®

Cathodic protection
Cathodic protection is a form of corrosion protection in which a more electrochemically active (anodic) metal is placed in electrical contact with the metal to be protected, so that the sacrificial metal corrodes in preference to the structural metal. In steel roofing, zinc (and zinc alloys in ZAM® and Galvalume) acts as the anode that sacrificially corrodes to protect the steel cathode. This means that even when the coating is breached at a cut edge, scratch or perforation, the surrounding zinc continues to provide cathodic protection to the exposed steel, preventing rust from spreading beneath the coating.
Barrier protection
Barrier protection is the second mechanism by which metallic coatings protect steel from corrosion — the physical barrier of the coating itself, which prevents moisture and oxygen from reaching the steel surface. The aluminium component of ZAM® and ZINCALUME® coatings forms a stable, dense aluminium oxide layer that acts as an excellent physical barrier. Barrier protection is most effective when the coating is intact; at cut edges or defects, cathodic protection takes over. The combination of both mechanisms in ZnAlMg alloys makes them significantly more durable than either mechanism alone.

See also: Cathodic protection · ZAM® coating

Coating weight
Coating weight (or coating mass) is the mass of metallic coating per unit area of steel surface, expressed in grams per square metre (g/m²) — typically measured as the total coating on both faces combined (e.g., Z275 = 275 g/m² total zinc, both sides). Heavier coatings provide longer service life but at greater cost and weight. Standard hot-dip galvanised steel for roofing typically carries Z275–Z450 coating; ZAM® and ZINCALUME® coatings achieve equivalent or superior protection at lower coating weights due to their alloy composition. BS EN 10346 defines coating designation classes for continuously hot-dip coated steel.

Surface Finish

Stone chip
Stone chip (or stone chip coating) is the decorative and protective surface layer applied to Metrotile steel tiles, consisting of crushed mineral aggregate — typically granite, quartzite, basalt or ceramic granules — factory-bonded to the metallic-coated steel substrate with an acrylic adhesive. The stone chip layer provides: UV protection (shielding the metallic coating from solar degradation); improved aesthetics (opaque, non-reflective, tactile surface resembling natural materials); grip for foot traffic during installation; and additional environmental protection. Stone chip particle size is calibrated to the tile profile and coverage specification. On Decra products, the stone chip is applied in a twice-baked process for enhanced adhesion.

Synonyms: stone chip coating · granule coating · mineral chip · aggregate coating

Acrylic binder
The acrylic binder (or acrylic adhesive layer) is the polymeric compound applied between the metallic-coated steel substrate and the stone chip aggregate to bond the granules firmly to the surface. The acrylic binder must be flexible enough to accommodate thermal expansion and contraction of the steel tile through daily and seasonal temperature cycling (typically −20°C to +80°C for a UK roof), whilst remaining adhesive over a 40-year service life. The formulation must also resist UV degradation, plasticiser migration and adhesion failure in wet-freeze-thaw cycling. Decra's twice-baked process uses two sequential acrylic applications with embedded stone chip between layers for exceptional bonding.
Twice-baked coating
Twice-baked coating is a premium stone chip application process used on Decra range tiles in which the acrylic binder and stone chip are applied and cured (baked) in two separate passes rather than one. In the first pass, a base acrylic layer is applied and partially cured; stone chip is then embedded into this layer and the assembly is cured again in a second baking pass, fully encapsulating the aggregate. The twice-baked method produces superior chip adhesion, colour depth and surface uniformity compared to single-application processes, and is a key quality differentiator for the Decra premium range.

See also: Decra Classic · Acrylic binder

Aggregate
In roofing tile terminology, aggregate refers to the mineral granules used in the stone chip coating — crushed granite, quartzite, ceramic or basalt particles that are sized, graded and pigmented to create the tile's colour and texture. Aggregate particle size is typically 1–4mm for most tile profiles. The aggregate material must be chemically inert (non-reactive with the acrylic binder or metallic coating), hard and wear-resistant, and UV-stable to retain colour throughout the product's design life. Pigmented ceramic aggregates are sometimes used for enhanced colour uniformity and UV stability.
Colour retention
Colour retention describes the ability of a roofing tile's surface finish to maintain its original colour, saturation and appearance over time despite exposure to UV radiation, weathering, freeze-thaw cycling and pollution. Metrotile stone chip tiles maintain good colour retention over their 40-year design life because the mineral pigmentation of the aggregate is intrinsic to the mineral structure rather than a surface paint, and is therefore not subject to chalking, peeling or fading in the same way as organic paint finishes. Colour comparisons at 10–20–40 years show minimal visual change for stone chip surfaces.
UV resistance
UV resistance refers to a roofing material's ability to withstand degradation caused by ultraviolet radiation from sunlight. UV energy can break down organic polymers (causing chalking, cracking and colour fading in painted or plastic products) and degrade adhesive bonds. Metrotile stone chip tiles are inherently UV-resistant because the opaque stone chip layer absorbs and scatters UV radiation before it reaches the acrylic binder beneath, protecting the binder from photodegradation. The metallic coating is also fully shielded from UV by the opaque stone chip layer.

Weight & Structural Properties

Tile weight
Tile weight (or tile self-weight) refers to the mass of the roofing tile per unit area (kg/m²), which directly determines the dead load imposed on the roof structure. Metrotile steel tiles have a self-weight of approximately 5.5–6.5 kg/m² (tile only), rising to approximately 7–8 kg/m² when battens, underlay, fixings and ridge/hip accessories are included. This compares favourably with:
  • Concrete interlocking tiles: 38–42 kg/m²
  • Clay plain tiles: 50–70 kg/m²
  • Natural slate: 22–32 kg/m²
  • Fibre cement slates: 16–18 kg/m²

See also: Dead load · Lightweight advantage

Dead load comparison
A dead load comparison quantifies the difference in structural loading imposed by different roof covering materials on the roof structure, expressed in kg/m² or kN/m². When replacing a heavy existing roof covering with Metrotile, the reduction in dead load typically eliminates the need for structural reinforcement of rafters, purlins and wall plates. For a 200m² roof, replacing concrete tiles (40 kg/m² = 80 kN total) with Metrotile (8 kg/m² = 16 kN total) reduces the structural dead load by approximately 64 kN — equivalent to removing 6.5 tonnes from the roof structure.

See also: Tile weight · Dead load

Lightweight advantage
The lightweight advantage of steel roofing tiles refers to the combination of structural, practical and commercial benefits that result from their low weight relative to traditional materials. Key advantages include: (1) no structural reinforcement required for most re-roofing projects over existing structures; (2) installation possible at pitches as low as 10°, where heavier tiles would be impractical; (3) suitability for conservatory roof conversion without structural modification; (4) reduced foundation loading on new build projects; (5) faster installation — fewer tiles per m², lighter to carry; (6) potential for overlay installation (new tiles directly over existing) in certain circumstances.
Thermal performance
The thermal performance of a roof covering describes its contribution to the overall thermal insulation of the building. Steel roofing tiles themselves have a very low thermal resistance (R-value) as the steel is a good conductor; the tile alone contributes minimally to roof insulation. However, because Metrotile's installation system is compatible with high-performance breathable underlays and does not require deep ventilation voids, the insulation layer in the roof construction can be maximised — typically achieving required U-values with less overall construction depth than traditional tiled roofs. Proper roof insulation specification must be made by a structural or building services engineer.

See also: U-value · R-value
