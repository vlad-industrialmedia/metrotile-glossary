Sustainability & Environment Glossary

Material & Carbon

Recycled Content
Recycled content is the proportion of a product's material that has been recovered from pre-consumer (manufacturing off-cuts) or post-consumer (end-of-life) waste streams and reprocessed into the new product. Steel is produced partly from scrap via electric arc furnaces, giving steel roofing tiles an inherent recycled content figure — typically 25–35% post-consumer recycled steel, depending on the production route. Specifiers use recycled content as a BREEAM Materials credit indicator and as evidence of responsible resource use in sustainability statements.

See also: Recyclability · Embodied Carbon

Recyclability
Recyclability describes a material's ability to be recovered, reprocessed, and reused at the end of its service life without loss of fundamental properties. Steel is one of the most recyclable materials on earth, with a global average recovery rate exceeding 85%. Metrotile steel tiles can be returned to the steel recycling supply chain when eventually replaced — diverting material from landfill and reducing the demand for virgin ore. This end-of-life benefit is captured in a lifecycle assessment as a credit in the final life-cycle stage (Module D under EN 15804).

Synonyms: End-of-life recovery · Recoverability

See also: Lifecycle Assessment · Circular Economy

Embodied Carbon
Embodied carbon is the total greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions — expressed as kg CO₂e — generated during the manufacture, transport, installation, maintenance, and disposal of a building product, excluding the energy used by the building in operation. It encompasses raw material extraction, processing, fabrication, and end-of-life treatment. For steel roofing tiles, embodied carbon is typically lower per square metre than fired clay or pre-cast concrete alternatives on a whole-life basis, particularly when the long service life and full recyclability of steel are accounted for. RIBA's 2030 Climate Challenge targets a 40% reduction in embodied carbon for new buildings by 2030.

See also: Operational Carbon · Whole-Life Carbon · LCA

Operational Carbon
Operational carbon is the GHG emissions produced by a building's energy consumption during its use — heating, cooling, lighting, and hot water. While the roof cladding itself does not directly generate operational carbon, it significantly influences it through thermal performance: a well-insulated, airtight roof assembly reduces heat loss and therefore lowers the building's heating demand and associated carbon emissions. Metrotile systems are compatible with high-performance insulation strategies that help minimise operational carbon over a building's lifetime.

See also: Embodied Carbon · Energy Efficiency

Whole-Life Carbon
Whole-life carbon is the sum of embodied carbon and operational carbon across the entire service life of a building or building element — from cradle (raw material extraction) to grave (disposal) or cradle to cradle (including recycling credits). The RICS Whole Life Carbon Assessment standard (2nd edition, 2023) provides a consistent methodology for calculating and reporting whole-life carbon in the UK. A product with higher upfront embodied carbon but very long service life and full recyclability may have a lower whole-life carbon profile than a seemingly greener alternative requiring frequent replacement.

See also: LCA · WLC

Low Embodied Energy
Low embodied energy describes materials and products that require relatively little energy to extract, process, and manufacture. Compared to fired clay tiles (which need kiln temperatures of 900–1,100 °C) and fibre cement, steel tiles produced via continuous cold-rolling after hot-dip galvanising have comparatively lower process energy per tonne. Embodied energy is expressed in MJ or GJ per kilogram or per square metre and is reported in an EPD or lifecycle assessment. Selecting low-embodied-energy materials is a key lever for reducing a project's overall carbon impact under BREEAM and Part L of the Building Regulations.

See also: Embodied Carbon · EPD

Responsible Sourcing
Responsible sourcing means procuring materials from supply chains that meet defined standards for environmental management, labour rights, and corporate governance. In the UK construction industry it is most commonly certified via BES 6001, which assesses the responsible sourcing of construction products across three tiers: corporate responsibility, supply-chain management, and responsible procurement. BREEAM awards Materials credits for products certified under BES 6001 or equivalent schemes. Metrotile's steel coil supply is sourced from major European mills operating to ISO 14001 environmental management standards.

Synonyms: Sustainable procurement

See also: BREEAM

Assessment & Reporting

Lifecycle Assessment (LCA)
Lifecycle assessment (LCA) is a systematic methodology for quantifying the environmental impacts of a product or system across its entire life — from raw material extraction (cradle) through manufacture, transport, use, maintenance, and end-of-life treatment (grave or cradle). In construction, LCA is governed by EN 15804 (Environmental Product Declarations for Construction Products) and ISO 14040/14044. It covers impacts including global warming potential (GWP/carbon), resource depletion, water use, ozone depletion, and eutrophication. An LCA is the data foundation for an EPD and for BREEAM Materials credits.

Synonyms: Life-cycle analysis

See also: EPD · Embodied Carbon

Environmental Product Declaration (EPD)
An Environmental Product Declaration (EPD) is a standardised, third-party verified document that transparently communicates the environmental performance of a construction product, based on a lifecycle assessment conducted in accordance with EN 15804 and an applicable product category rule (PCR). EPDs report impact categories including global warming potential (GWP), ozone depletion potential, and resource consumption across lifecycle stages (A1–A5 production and construction, B1–B7 use stage, C1–C4 end of life, D beyond-boundary benefits). EPDs are required for BREEAM Mat 01 credits and are increasingly requested in public-sector specifications under PAS 2080 and RIBA Stages 2–3 sustainability reports.

See also: LCA · BREEAM

Carbon Footprint
Carbon footprint is the total quantity of greenhouse gas emissions caused by an activity, organisation, product, or event, expressed in CO₂ equivalent (CO₂e). For a building product, carbon footprint is typically the global warming potential (GWP) figure extracted from an EPD or LCA. Steel tiles have a carbon footprint that must be considered in context: while steel production is energy-intensive, the longevity (40+ years), low maintenance, and 100% recyclability of the finished tile significantly improve the whole-life carbon position compared with shorter-lived alternatives.

See also: Embodied Carbon · Whole-Life Carbon

Whole-Life Cost (WLC)
Whole-life cost (WLC), also called lifecycle cost (LCC) or through-life cost, is the net present value of all costs associated with owning and operating a building element from initial construction through maintenance, repair, replacement, and eventual disposal. For roofing, WLC analysis demonstrates the economic advantage of durable, low-maintenance systems: a lightweight steel tile roof with a 40-year guaranteed service life and negligible maintenance requirements compares favourably against concrete or clay tiles that may require repointing, moss treatment, or replacement within that period. HM Treasury's Green Book methodology and BS ISO 15686-5 provide WLC frameworks widely used in UK public-sector procurement.

Synonyms: Lifecycle cost · Through-life cost

See also: Whole-Life Carbon

Volatile Organic Compound (VOC)
Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are organic chemicals that evaporate readily at room temperature and can affect indoor air quality and contribute to photochemical smog. In roofing, VOC considerations arise primarily in coating systems, adhesives, and sealants. Metrotile's factory-applied PVDF and ZAM® coatings are fully cured before delivery, meaning no significant VOC off-gassing occurs on site. BREEAM Hea 02 (Indoor Air Quality) and BS EN 15251 set limits for acceptable VOC levels in occupied spaces; low-VOC products help specifiers meet these credits and comply with Part F (Ventilation) of the Building Regulations.

Synonyms: VOC emissions

Green Building Standards

BREEAM
BREEAM (Building Research Establishment Environmental Assessment Method) is the world's leading sustainability assessment framework for buildings, developed by the BRE in 1990. BREEAM rates buildings across nine categories — Management, Health & Wellbeing, Energy, Transport, Water, Materials, Waste, Land Use & Ecology, and Pollution — and awards ratings from Pass to Outstanding. Lightweight steel roofing can contribute credits in Materials (recycled content, responsible sourcing, EPD, lifecycle performance, design for disassembly), Energy (thermal performance), and Waste (reduced construction waste from lightweight, pre-formed tiles). BREEAM is mandatory on many UK commercial, public-sector, and housing association projects.

See also: EPD · Responsible Sourcing

Passivhaus
Passivhaus (German: passive house) is an ultra-low-energy building standard developed by the Passivhaus Institut (PHI) in Darmstadt that specifies rigorous performance criteria for space heating demand (≤15 kWh/m²/yr), total primary energy demand (≤120 kWh/m²/yr), airtightness (≤0.6 ACH at 50 Pa), and thermal comfort. Achieving Passivhaus requires a continuous, unbroken envelope of insulation and airtightness layers — a challenge for roof junctions. Lightweight steel tiles are compatible with Passivhaus roof assemblies when installed over a correctly detailed breathable membrane, service cavity, and continuous insulation layer without thermal bridges through the tile fixings.

See also: EnerPHit · Thermal Mass

EnerPHit
EnerPHit is the Passivhaus Institut's certification standard for retrofit and refurbishment projects that cannot fully achieve Passivhaus criteria due to existing structural or planning constraints. EnerPHit allows a slightly relaxed heating demand limit (25 kWh/m²/yr with the energy demand method, or prescribed component U-values via the component method) while still targeting very high airtightness and thermal comfort. Re-roofing with a lightweight steel tile system during an EnerPHit refurbishment offers an opportunity to upgrade roof insulation, improve airtightness at eaves and ridge details, and add solar-integrated tiles without overloading the existing structure.

See also: Passivhaus

Net Zero
Net zero refers to achieving a balance between greenhouse gas emissions produced and emissions removed from the atmosphere, so that the net addition to atmospheric CO₂e is zero. The UK government's legally binding target is net zero by 2050 under the Climate Change Act 2008 (as amended). In the built environment context, net zero encompasses both operational carbon (addressed through energy efficiency and renewables) and increasingly embodied carbon (addressed through material selection, lifetime extension, and recyclability). Long-lived, fully recyclable steel tile roofs contribute to net-zero strategies by extending building lifespans, reducing replacement frequency, and enabling end-of-life material recovery.

See also: Decarbonisation · Whole-Life Carbon

Decarbonisation
Decarbonisation is the process of reducing or eliminating carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gas emissions from an activity or sector. In the UK construction industry, decarbonisation of the built environment encompasses improving the energy efficiency of existing building stock, reducing embodied carbon in new construction, transitioning to low-carbon heating systems, and integrating renewable energy generation. Specifying durable, low-maintenance roofing materials with low embodied carbon, high recyclability, and solar integration capability (such as the Metrotile eQube Solar Tile) supports the decarbonisation agenda across multiple pathways simultaneously.

Circular Economy
The circular economy is an economic model that aims to eliminate waste and keep materials in use for as long as possible at their highest value, in contrast to the traditional linear model of take-make-dispose. For construction products, circular economy principles include designing for disassembly (tiles can be removed individually and reused), using recycled and recyclable materials (steel), extending service life through durability and repairability, and recovering material at end of life. BREEAM Mat 06 (Material Efficiency) specifically rewards products designed for disassembly and material recovery. Metrotile's mechanically fixed steel tile system is inherently compatible with these principles.

See also: Recyclability · BREEAM

Building & Site Performance

Thermal Mass
Thermal mass is a material's ability to absorb, store, and slowly release thermal energy, moderating temperature swings inside a building. High-thermal-mass materials (concrete, masonry, water) absorb heat during the day and release it at night, improving comfort and reducing peak cooling loads in summer. Steel roofing tiles have low thermal mass compared with concrete or clay alternatives — they absorb and release heat quickly. In a well-insulated roof assembly, this has minimal practical impact because the insulation layer decouples the tile from the interior environment. Where thermal mass is a design priority, it is better provided by the building's structure and floor slab.

See also: Passivhaus · Energy Efficiency

Energy Efficiency
Energy efficiency in buildings means delivering the required thermal comfort, lighting, and services with the minimum energy input. For the roof, energy efficiency is primarily delivered by the insulation specification (achieving the required U-value, typically 0.16–0.20 W/m²K for new build under Part L), airtightness of the membrane and junctions, and elimination of thermal bridges at rafters, ridges, and eaves. A lightweight tile roof imposes lower structural loads than concrete or clay, potentially enabling thicker insulation in slimmer structural sections, which improves whole-roof energy performance. Metrotile systems are compatible with all mainstream insulation strategies including mineral wool, PIR, and spray foam.

Urban Heat Island Effect
The urban heat island (UHI) effect is the phenomenon whereby built-up urban areas experience significantly higher temperatures than surrounding rural areas, due to dark hard surfaces absorbing solar radiation, reduced vegetation, and waste heat from buildings and vehicles. Roof surfaces are a major contributor: dark, low-reflectance materials (such as bituminous felt or some concrete tiles) absorb and re-emit heat, elevating local ambient temperatures. Lighter-coloured roofing materials with higher solar reflectance index (SRI) values can mitigate UHI. Metrotile tiles are available in lighter stone chip colours that reflect more solar radiation, contributing to urban cooling strategies increasingly required in planning policy.

Synonyms: Heat island effect · UHI

Green Roof
A green roof (living roof) is a roof system that incorporates a growing medium and planted vegetation layer over a waterproof membrane, providing benefits including biodiversity net gain, stormwater attenuation, urban cooling, and additional roof insulation. Green roofs are classified as extensive (thin substrate, 60–150 mm, sedum/mosses, low maintenance) or intensive (deeper substrate, full landscaping, accessible). Lightweight steel tile systems and green roofs serve different architectural contexts, but both are tools in the urban sustainability toolkit. On re-roofing projects where green roofs are not structurally feasible, the lightweight nature of steel tiles (7–8 kg/m²) makes them a viable alternative that does not add load to ageing roof structures.

See also: SuDS

Sustainable Drainage System (SuDS)
A sustainable drainage system (SuDS) is an approach to managing rainwater runoff that mimics natural drainage, reducing flood risk and improving water quality by slowing, storing, infiltrating, or treating water close to where it falls. SuDS features include permeable paving, rain gardens, swales, detention basins, and green roofs. Roof run-off is a significant contributor to urban surface water flood risk: the roof catchment area and its surface finish determine run-off volume and peak flow rate. While steel tiles are impermeable, their profile geometry (channels and interlocks) controls run-off direction and speed, and sizing gutters and downpipes to match the roof's calculated run-off (to BS EN 12056-3) is integral to a site's SuDS strategy.

Synonyms: SUDS · Sustainable urban drainage

See also: Rainwater Harvesting

Rainwater Harvesting
Rainwater harvesting (RWH) is the collection, storage, and reuse of rainwater from roof surfaces, typically for non-potable uses such as toilet flushing, garden irrigation, and vehicle washing. The roof cladding material affects RWH suitability: surfaces must be free from soluble chemicals, heavy metals, or coatings that could contaminate collected water. Metrotile tiles with stone chip finish and ZAM®-coated steel substrate have been used with rainwater harvesting systems; the coating is insoluble under normal exposure conditions. BS 8515 provides UK guidance on designing and installing rainwater harvesting systems. BREEAM Wat 02 (Water Monitoring) and Wat 04 (Water Efficient Equipment) can award credits where RWH reduces mains water consumption.

Synonyms: RWH · Roof water harvesting

See also: SuDS
