Roof Types & Configurations

This section defines 26 common roof types and geometric configurations found in UK residential, conservatory, commercial and agricultural construction. Understanding roof form is essential when specifying roofing materials, calculating areas and pitches, and planning structural loading. All roof types listed here can be clad with Metrotile lightweight steel tiles subject to minimum pitch requirements.

Simple Pitched Roofs

Gable Roof
A gable roof is the most common roof form in the UK — two sloping planes meeting at a central ridge, with vertical triangular gable walls at each end. It is structurally straightforward, easy to construct, and provides excellent roof space. The gable ends may be clad in tile-hanging, timber boarding, render or brickwork. Gable roofs are compatible with all Metrotile profiles and are the standard starting point for most new-build and re-roofing projects.

See also: Dual pitch · Gable (structural term)

→ Metrotile steel tiles for gable roofs

Hip Roof
A hip roof has four sloping sides — two triangular hip ends and two trapezoidal main slopes — all meeting at a central ridge. There are no vertical gable ends. Hip roofs are more aerodynamically stable than gable roofs and are preferred in high-wind and coastal exposure zones. The hip intersections are formed with Metrotile hip caps and hip soakers, and can be finished dry-fix with no mortar. A half-hip (hipped gable or Jerkinhead) blends both forms.

See also: Hipped gable · Hip rafter · Hip cap

→ Hip caps & accessories

Dual Pitch
Dual pitch (also called a double-pitch or symmetrical pitched roof) describes any roof with two slopes of equal or similar pitch meeting at a ridge — the standard gable and hip roof forms. The term is often used in contrast to mono-pitch when specifying or designing extensions. Both slopes are tiled identically, and ridge length, hip length and valley length are calculated from the roof's plan dimensions and pitch angle.

Synonyms: Double pitch · Symmetrical pitch

Mono-Pitch Roof
A mono-pitch roof has a single sloping plane — one ridge or high edge, one eaves or low edge, no central ridge or valley. It is the simplest roof form to build and is commonly used for extensions, garages, sheds, industrial buildings and contemporary new build. The minimum pitch for Metrotile on a mono-pitch is 10°, and low-pitch profiles such as Aquapan can be used from 10°. Mono-pitch roofs require careful detailing at the high abutment and verge edges to prevent wind-driven rain ingress.

Synonyms: Shed roof · Single-pitch · Single-slope

→ Aquapan — low-pitch profile

Lean-To Roof
A lean-to roof is a mono-pitch roof that abuts and is supported by an existing wall of a taller structure — typically an extension, outbuilding or conservatory added to the side or rear of a house. The high edge of the lean-to is fixed to the existing wall via a flashing and abutment detail, and the low edge forms the eaves. Lean-to roofs are a very common conservatory form (lean-to conservatory) and are routinely reclad with Metrotile as part of solid conservatory roof conversions.

See also: Lean-to conservatory · Abutment flashing

→ Conservatory roof conversions

Skillion Roof
A skillion roof is a modern architectural term for a mono-pitch or asymmetrical shed roof with a pronounced single slope, typically used in contemporary and Modernist residential design. The term originates from Australian building terminology but is now widely used in UK architectural practice. Skillion roofs often feature dramatic overhanging eaves, exposed rafters and minimal roof thickness. When clad with Metrotile flat or slate-profile tiles, they achieve a clean low-profile appearance.

Synonyms: Shed roof · Mono-pitch · Cat-slide (when extending from main ridge to eaves level)

Complex & Compound Roof Forms

Hipped Gable (Jerkinhead)
A hipped gable (also called a Jerkinhead or half-hip) is a hybrid roof form: the main slopes are as a gable roof, but the gable ends are cut short and capped with small triangular hips. The result is a partial hip where the top of the gable end is sloped, while the lower portion remains vertical. Hipped gables are common on traditional British cottages and Arts & Crafts houses and perform better in high winds than full gable roofs. The hip-gable junction requires carefully cut tiles and a mitred hip finish.

Synonyms: Jerkinhead · Half-hip · Clipped gable

Gambrel Roof
A gambrel roof has two slopes on each side — a steep lower slope and a shallower upper slope — creating additional headroom and usable loft space. The form is common in Dutch Colonial and American barn architecture and is the traditional shape of an English cottage with a steeply pitched lower slope. In the UK the term is sometimes used interchangeably with mansard, though strictly a gambrel has gable ends while a mansard has hips on all four sides. Metrotile tiles perform well on both the upper and lower slopes.

See also: Mansard roof

Mansard Roof
A mansard roof has four sides, each with a double slope — a steep lower slope (often 70–80°) and a shallow upper slope (15–30°). The steep lower slopes maximise habitable attic space; the shallow upper slopes are often barely visible from street level. Mansard roofs are common in Victorian and Edwardian terraces and are a popular vehicle for loft conversions in London. The steep lower slopes are frequently tiled, and Metrotile steel tiles are an excellent choice given their light weight and weathering performance. The term derives from the French architect François Mansart.

See also: Gambrel · Loft conversion

Butterfly Roof
A butterfly roof is an inverted V-shape — two roof planes sloping downward toward a central valley rather than rising to a ridge. The form is associated with mid-century Modernist architecture and is used to create dramatic interior ceiling lines and capture rainwater in the central valley. Butterfly roofs require robust valley drainage and waterproofing. They are uncommon in UK residential construction but occasionally used in contemporary self-build projects.

Synonyms: Inverted gable · V-roof

Sawtooth Roof
A sawtooth roof is a series of parallel mono-pitch slopes resembling the teeth of a saw, typically with vertical or near-vertical glazed north-facing faces alternating with shallower south-facing tiled slopes. Sawtooth roofs are most common on factory and industrial buildings where they provide consistent north-facing daylight without direct sun glare. The tiled slopes can be clad with Metrotile steel tiles, while the vertical or near-vertical glazed faces use purpose-made aluminium or steel cladding.

Synonyms: North-light roof · Monitor roof (when the glazed faces are at both the top and bottom of each tooth)

Pyramid Roof
A pyramid roof is a hip roof with all four slopes meeting at a single apex point, with no ridge. It is used on square or nearly square plan forms such as towers, garages, porches, summer houses and agricultural buildings. The apex requires a special cap or lead soaker detail. All four hip intersections are formed with hip cap tiles or mitred ridge tiles. Metrotile tiles are well suited to small pyramid roof structures, especially where the structure cannot support the weight of concrete or clay tiles.

See also: Hip roof

Dormers & Roof Extensions

Dormer
A dormer is a structural element projecting vertically from a sloping roof plane, typically containing a window and covered by its own small roof. Dormers increase headroom and usable floor area in attic and loft spaces, and improve natural light and ventilation in roof-level rooms. The dormer cheeks (side walls) are usually tiled or clad, and the dormer roof may be gable, hip, flat or arched. Metrotile steel tiles are commonly used on dormer roofs and cheeks in loft conversions.

See also: Gable dormer · Hip dormer · Shed dormer

→ Dormer loft conversions

Gable Dormer
A gable dormer is a dormer with a small gable roof — two slopes meeting at a ridge running perpendicular to the main roof slope. It is the most traditional dormer form in UK domestic architecture and is common on Victorian, Edwardian and Arts & Crafts houses. The gable end of a gable dormer typically features a barge board and may be open or closed. Tile-for-tile matching between the dormer roof and the main roof is possible with Metrotile as the same profile and colour is used on both.
Hip Dormer
A hip dormer is a dormer with a hipped roof — three slopes converging at a single ridge without a gable end. Hip dormers are more compact in appearance than gable dormers and are well suited to hipped main roofs where a gable would look incongruous. They require careful hip soaker and cap detailing at the junction with the main roof.
Shed Dormer
A shed dormer (also called a flat-roof dormer in UK planning terminology) is a dormer with a single mono-pitch slope, usually at a very low pitch or near-horizontal. Shed dormers typically run the full or most of the width of the roof and provide maximum headroom over the loft floor. They are the most common dormer type used in UK loft conversions. The mono-pitch dormer roof requires careful waterproofing at the low pitch; Metrotile low-pitch profiles can be used on pitches down to 10°.

Synonyms: Flat-roof dormer · Box dormer

Eyebrow Dormer
An eyebrow dormer is a small curved or wave-shaped dormer set flush into the main roof slope without a vertical face — the roof covering flows continuously over the top of the dormer in a gentle curve, with only a small curved window visible. Eyebrow dormers are characteristic of Arts & Crafts and Vernacular revival architecture and are complex to construct and tile. They are generally not suitable for Metrotile steel tiles due to the compound curved geometry.

Synonyms: Cat-slide dormer · Wave dormer

Loft Conversion
A loft conversion is the process of converting an existing unused roof space into a habitable room, typically a bedroom or office. Common loft conversion types include Velux (roof windows only), dormer (one or more dormers added), hip-to-gable (a hip end extended to create a gable), and full mansard. Loft conversions are often an opportunity to re-roof or re-clad the entire roof with a lightweight material such as Metrotile, which minimises the additional structural load on the existing timbers.

See also: Dormer · Mansard

Flat & Conversion Roofs

Flat Roof
A flat roof is a roof with a very shallow pitch — typically less than 10°, often 1–3° (a fall rather than a true pitch) — designed to be waterproofed with a continuous membrane or built-up roofing system rather than overlapping tiles. Flat roofs are common on post-war domestic extensions, industrial buildings and commercial premises. They are notorious for leaks and short lifespans compared to pitched roofs. Converting a flat roof to a pitched roof using Metrotile is one of the most cost-effective ways to eliminate flat-roof maintenance problems.

See also: Flat-to-pitch conversion

Flat-to-Pitch Conversion
Flat-to-pitch conversion (also called a pitched roof overlay or pitched roof-over) is the process of adding a pitched roof structure over an existing flat roof. A lightweight timber or steel framework is erected on top of the flat roof, battens are installed at the correct gauge, and a new pitched roof covering is fixed on top. The existing flat roof deck is typically retained as a structural base. Because Metrotile tiles weigh only 7–8 kg/m², they are the material of choice for flat-to-pitch conversions where the existing structure has limited spare load capacity. No planning permission is required for many domestic flat-to-pitch conversions as permitted development.

Synonyms: Pitched roof overlay · Roof-over · Pitch-over

→ Flat-to-pitch conversions

Low-Pitch Roof
A low-pitch roof is a pitched roof with a slope angle below approximately 17.5°, which is the minimum pitch for most traditional clay and concrete interlocking tiles. Low-pitch roofs present waterproofing challenges as the tile overlap must be increased and each course must be more carefully lapped to prevent wind-driven rain from travelling uphill beneath the tiles. Metrotile profiles have been specifically designed and independently tested (BBA 07/4470) for installation at pitches down to 10°, making them the preferred choice for low-pitch applications.

See also: Minimum pitch · Aquapan

Warm Roof
A warm roof is an insulated roof construction in which the thermal insulation is placed above the structural deck, keeping the structure warm and dry. It is the preferred modern approach for flat and low-pitch roofs because it eliminates the risk of interstitial condensation within the roof structure. In a warm roof, the insulation layer is followed by a waterproofing layer; if a pitched tile finish is required, the insulation layer is followed by counter-battens and tiling battens. Contrast with cold roof.

See also: Cold roof · U-value

Cold Roof
A cold roof is an insulated roof construction in which the thermal insulation is placed at ceiling level (between or below the ceiling joists), leaving the roof void above uninsulated and cold. Cold roofs rely on ventilation of the roof void to prevent condensation on the cold underside of the roof deck. They are common in traditional UK domestic construction (insulation in the loft floor) but are increasingly discouraged for new construction in favour of warm roofs. Where a cold roof is converted to a habitable loft room, the insulation must be moved to rafter level, creating a warm roof.

Contrast: Warm roof

Pitch, Geometry & Span

Pitch Angle
Pitch angle is the angle of a roof slope measured in degrees from the horizontal. It is the primary geometric parameter for specifying roofing materials, as minimum pitch requirements are set by the tile manufacturer and independently verified by the BBA. Common domestic pitches are 30–45°; low-pitch roofs are typically 10–17.5°; flat roofs have pitches below 5°. Pitch can also be expressed as a ratio (rise:run, e.g. 1:3) or as a percentage (rise as a percentage of run). To convert degrees to ratio: tan(pitch°) = rise/run.

See also: Minimum pitch

Roof Span
Roof span is the horizontal distance across the full width of the roof from external wall face to external wall face (or from rafter foot to rafter foot). It is the key dimension for sizing rafters, purlins and roof trusses. Half the span (the horizontal distance from eaves to the centre line) is the half-span, which is used together with the pitch angle to calculate the rafter length and the roof rise. Span is also the input to standard rafter span tables in BS EN 1995 and TRADA span tables.

See also: Roof rise · Rafter

Roof Rise
Roof rise is the vertical height from the top of the wall plate (eaves level) to the underside of the ridge. Together with the span, it defines the pitch angle: tan(pitch) = rise / (span/2). A steeper pitch produces a greater rise for a given span and increases the usable loft volume. Rise is a key parameter in loft conversion feasibility assessments — a minimum headroom of approximately 2.0 m is needed at the ridge for a usable loft room.

See also: Pitch angle · Roof span

Exposure Category
Exposure category classifies the wind-driven rain and wind load severity at a building site. UK roofing standards (BS 5534, BBA certificates) use exposure categories — typically Sheltered, Moderate, Severe and Very Severe — to determine the required tile fixing specification (number of nails or clips per tile) and minimum pitch. Coastal, upland and west-facing sites in the UK typically fall into Severe or Very Severe categories. Metrotile BBA Certificate 07/4470 specifies fixing requirements for each exposure category.

See also: BBA Certificate · Wind uplift

Roof Area
Roof area is the actual sloping surface area of the roof, measured on the slope rather than in plan. It is calculated from the plan area multiplied by the slope factor (1/cos(pitch°)). Roof area is the key quantity for calculating the quantity of tiles, underlays, battens and accessories required. For a gable roof with a 30° pitch, the slope factor is 1/cos(30°) = 1.155, so a 100 m² plan area produces 115.5 m² of actual roof area. Metrotile tiles are ordered by m² of installed area.

See also: Pitch angle
