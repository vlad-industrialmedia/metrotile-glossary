BIPV & Solar Tiles

eQube Solar Tile
The Metrotile eQube Solar Tile is a building-integrated photovoltaic (BIPV) roofing tile that combines Metrotile's proven interlocking lightweight steel tile system with a solar PV cell layer, generating electricity while simultaneously acting as the primary weatherproof roof cladding. The eQube integrates flush with the surrounding standard Metrotile tiles, delivering an architecturally discrete solar roof that maintains the visual character of a conventional tiled roof without the visual bulk or structural penetrations of rack-mounted solar panel arrays. The system is designed for both new-build and re-roofing applications, and is compatible with standard grid-connect inverters and battery storage systems. As a BIPV product, the eQube may qualify as Permitted Development where rack-mounted panels might not, particularly on buildings where protrusion above the roof line would require planning consent.

BIPV (Building-Integrated Photovoltaics)
Building-integrated photovoltaics (BIPV) describes solar PV technology incorporated directly into a building's external envelope — replacing or substituting conventional building materials such as roof tiles, wall cladding, spandrel glass, or façade panels. Unlike BAPV (building-applied or bolt-on PV panels), BIPV products serve dual functions: generating electricity and providing the primary weatherproofing or structural skin of the building envelope. BIPV offers several advantages: it eliminates the visual intrusion of rack-mounted arrays; it avoids the additional roof loadings and penetrations of BAPV fixings; it may qualify for Permitted Development in contexts where BAPV does not; and it simplifies programme by integrating power generation into the roofing installation. The Metrotile eQube is a tile-integrated BIPV product operating at the residential and light commercial scale.

Synonyms: Integrated solar roofing · Solar roofing tile

See also: eQube Solar Tile · Photovoltaic

Solar Shingle
A solar shingle is a BIPV roofing product in which a photovoltaic cell is incorporated into a tile or shingle form factor — either overlapping or interlocking — so that the solar element is indistinguishable from the surrounding conventional roof covering at normal viewing distances. Solar shingles were pioneered by Tesla's Solar Roof (glass tiles), SunRoof, and RGS Energy's Powerhouse shingles in the US market. The Metrotile eQube Solar Tile is the UK market's steel-tile BIPV equivalent, using the Metrotile interlock and steel substrate to achieve a flush, weathertight integration. Solar shingles generally have lower peak watt output per unit area than conventional panels (due to smaller cell size), but generate electricity from the entire roof plane rather than a discrete array.

See also: BIPV · eQube Solar Tile

Photovoltaic (PV)
Photovoltaic (PV) refers to the physical process — and the technology derived from it — by which semiconductor materials (most commonly silicon) convert photons of light directly into direct current (DC) electrical energy. The photovoltaic effect was discovered by Edmond Becquerel in 1839 and commercialised from the 1950s onwards. Modern PV cells are classified by technology: monocrystalline silicon (highest efficiency, 20–24% for commercial cells), polycrystalline silicon (lower efficiency, lower cost), and thin-film technologies (amorphous silicon, CIGS, CdTe — lower efficiency but lighter, flexible, and suitable for BIPV integration into curved or irregular substrates). Solar irradiance, cell temperature, and shading all affect the conversion efficiency and real-world output of a PV installation.

Synonyms: PV · Solar PV · Solar electric

See also: kWp · Solar Irradiance

PV Panel (Solar Panel)
A PV panel (solar panel or solar module) is a factory-assembled unit of interconnected PV cells encapsulated in glass or polymer, forming the basic building block of a solar array. Standard residential panels are typically 60- or 72-cell monocrystalline modules rated at 350–450 Wp. In conventional BAPV installations, panels are mounted on aluminium rail systems above the roof covering. BIPV products like the Metrotile eQube Solar Tile replace the conventional panel form factor with a tile-form factor that integrates into the roofing system itself, eliminating rails and brackets. Output from multiple panels or tiles is aggregated through DC wiring to a central or micro-inverter.

Synonyms: Solar module · PV module

See also: BIPV · Inverter

Inverter
An inverter is the electronic device that converts the direct current (DC) electricity generated by PV panels or tiles into alternating current (AC) electricity compatible with the building's electrical system and the national grid. Types include: string inverters (single unit converting output from multiple panels in series — simple, cost-effective, but vulnerable to shading on any one panel); micro-inverters (one per panel or tile — more expensive but each unit optimises independently, improving shading tolerance and enabling granular monitoring); and power optimisers (panel-level MPPT optimisation combined with a central string inverter). For BIPV solar tile systems like the eQube, micro-inverters are often preferred because their tile-by-tile optimisation compensates for the varied orientations and potential shading of a complex tile roof.

Synonyms: Solar inverter · Grid-connect inverter

See also: On-Grid System

Performance & Output

kWp (Kilowatt-Peak)
kWp (kilowatt-peak) is the unit used to express the rated power output of a solar PV system under standard test conditions (STC): 1,000 W/m² irradiance, 25 °C cell temperature, and AM1.5 solar spectrum. It represents the maximum instantaneous power output the system can theoretically achieve and is used to size and compare solar installations. Actual energy output (kWh/year) depends on the site's solar resource (irradiance), orientation, pitch, shading, and inverter efficiency. A typical south-facing UK roof at 35° pitch with 4 kWp installed capacity will generate approximately 3,400–3,800 kWh/year. For BIPV solar tile systems, the kWp is calculated from the number of solar tiles and their individual rated watt-peak (Wp) output.

See also: Solar Irradiance · Solar Fraction

Solar Irradiance
Solar irradiance is the power of solar radiation received per unit area of a surface, expressed in watts per square metre (W/m²). It varies with latitude, time of day, season, cloud cover, and atmospheric conditions. In the UK, horizontal global irradiance ranges from approximately 900–1,100 kWh/m²/year in northern Scotland to 1,100–1,300 kWh/m²/year in southern England. For PV system design, the irradiance on the tilted roof surface at the proposed installation angle and orientation (azimuth) is calculated using tools such as PVGIS (Photovoltaic Geographical Information System) or SAP 2012's solar irradiance tables. South-facing roofs at 30–40° pitch receive approximately 15–20% more irradiance than horizontal surfaces in the UK.

Synonyms: Solar radiation · Insolation

See also: kWp · Solar Orientation

Solar Fraction
Solar fraction is the proportion of a building's total annual energy demand (heating, hot water, or electricity, depending on context) that is met by on-site solar energy generation. A solar fraction of 0.40 (40%) means that 40% of the relevant energy demand is met by solar, with the remaining 60% supplied by the grid or another source. For solar PV systems, the solar fraction depends on the installed capacity (kWp), the building's electricity demand profile, and the self-consumption rate (the proportion of solar generation used on-site rather than exported). Maximising solar fraction — through combination of solar generation, battery storage, and demand management — is a key strategy for achieving net-zero operational carbon.

See also: Self-Consumption · Battery Storage

Solar Orientation & Azimuth
Solar orientation is the compass bearing (azimuth) of a roof slope relative to south — the direction that maximises solar yield in the northern hemisphere. A roof facing directly south has an azimuth of 0°; east-facing is −90°, west-facing +90°. In the UK, south-facing roofs at pitches of 30–40° typically capture 95–100% of maximum achievable yield; south-east and south-west orientations (±45°) capture approximately 90–95%; east and west-facing roofs capture 70–85%. BIPV solar tile systems like the eQube can be installed on any roof slope, but yield modelling for non-south-facing slopes is important to set realistic output expectations. Shading from adjacent buildings, trees, and roof features (dormers, chimneys) must be assessed alongside orientation.

Synonyms: Azimuth · Roof orientation

See also: Solar Irradiance · Shading

Shading & Yield Loss
Shading on a PV installation reduces its electricity output, potentially disproportionately — because in a standard string inverter system, the performance of the weakest (most shaded) panel limits the output of the entire string. Sources of shading include: chimneys, dormer windows, and satellite dishes on the same roof; trees and neighbouring buildings; overhead cables; and soiling (dust, moss, bird droppings). Shading assessment is performed using tools such as the Solar Pathfinder, Solmetric SunEye, or 3D modelling software. Micro-inverters, employed in BIPV solar tile systems, mitigate the impact of partial shading by allowing each tile to operate independently at its maximum power point (MPPT), so shading on one tile does not disproportionately reduce the output of others.

See also: Orientation · Inverter

Self-Consumption
Self-consumption is the proportion of a solar PV system's total annual generation that is used directly within the building — heating, lighting, appliances, electric vehicle charging — rather than exported to the grid. A higher self-consumption rate is economically beneficial: self-consumed electricity saves the retail electricity price (currently ~25–30 p/kWh), while exported electricity earns only the SEG tariff (typically 4–20 p/kWh). Self-consumption rates for residential systems in the UK range from approximately 25–40% for daytime-absent households to 50–70% for work-from-home households. Adding battery storage significantly increases self-consumption by shifting solar generation to evening demand hours, improving the economics of the installation.

See also: Solar Fraction · Battery Storage · SEG

Policy & Grid

MCS Certification
MCS (Microgeneration Certification Scheme) is the UK's quality standard framework for small-scale renewable energy technologies (solar PV, heat pumps, wind turbines) and their installers. MCS certification of the installation contractor is required for the customer to access the Smart Export Guarantee (SEG) and most domestic renewable energy finance products and grants. Products used in MCS-certified installations must also be MCS-approved (listed on the MCS product database). MCS standards for solar PV installations include MCS 012 (product standard for solar PV panels) and MCS 001/MIS 3002 (installation standard). Metrotile's eQube Solar Tile should be installed by an MCS-certified contractor to qualify for SEG payments and applicable government incentives.

See also: SEG · Microgeneration

Smart Export Guarantee (SEG)
The Smart Export Guarantee (SEG) is the UK government's current mechanism for rewarding small-scale renewable electricity generators who export surplus electricity to the national grid. Introduced in January 2020 to replace the closed Feed-In Tariff (FIT) scheme, the SEG requires Ofgem-licensed electricity suppliers with 150,000 or more domestic customers to offer at least one SEG tariff — a guaranteed payment per kWh of electricity exported. SEG tariff rates are set by suppliers (not the government), typically ranging from 4–20 p/kWh, and must be greater than zero at all times. To qualify, the installation must be 5 MW or smaller, MCS-certified, fitted with a smart meter capable of half-hourly export measurement, and the customer must not already receive a FIT generation tariff on the same installation.

Synonyms: Export tariff

See also: Feed-In Tariff · MCS

Feed-In Tariff (FIT)
The Feed-In Tariff (FIT) was the UK government's first major incentive scheme for small-scale renewable electricity generation, operating from April 2010 to March 2019 (closed to new applicants). Under FIT, eligible customers received a generation tariff (payment per kWh generated, whether used on-site or exported) and an export tariff (payment for electricity exported to the grid, typically calculated at 50% of generation as a deemed export). Rates were index-linked and guaranteed for 20 years from the installation date. Existing FIT recipients (pre-March 2019 installations) continue to receive FIT payments for the remainder of their 20-year tariff period. New solar PV installations after March 2019 access the SEG for export payments instead.

Synonyms: Solar FIT

See also: SEG

Microgeneration
Microgeneration is the small-scale production of heat or electricity from renewable sources at or near the point of use — typically defined in the UK as systems up to 50 kW electrical or 45 kW thermal output. Common microgeneration technologies include solar PV, solar thermal, heat pumps, micro-wind turbines, micro-hydro, and micro-CHP. The Microgeneration Certification Scheme (MCS) provides quality standards for microgeneration products and installers. BIPV solar tile systems fall squarely within the microgeneration category, typically ranging from 2–10 kWp for residential installations. The Domestic Renewable Heat Incentive (RHI) has been replaced by the Boiler Upgrade Scheme (BUS) for heat technologies; solar PV export is incentivised through the SEG.

See also: MCS · kWp

On-Grid (Grid-Connected) System
A grid-connected (on-grid) solar PV system is connected to the public electricity network, allowing it to import electricity from the grid when solar generation is insufficient and export surplus solar electricity when generation exceeds demand. Grid connection is the standard configuration for residential and commercial solar PV in the UK. The inverter synchronises with the grid frequency (50 Hz in the UK) and automatically shuts down if the grid fails (anti-islanding protection), protecting utility workers during grid maintenance. A grid-connected system requires a G99 (systems over 16 A per phase) or G98 (16 A per phase or less) notification or application to the Distribution Network Operator (DNO) before connection.

Synonyms: Grid-tied system · Utility-interactive system

See also: Off-Grid · G99 / G98

Off-Grid System
An off-grid (stand-alone) solar PV system operates independently of the public electricity network, relying entirely on on-site generation (solar, wind, micro-hydro) and battery storage (and typically a diesel or petrol generator as backup) to meet all electrical demand. Off-grid systems are appropriate for remote locations where grid connection is cost-prohibitive or technically impractical — agricultural buildings, remote holiday lodges, remote monitoring stations. They are technically more complex than grid-connected systems, requiring careful demand management, battery sizing to cover days of low irradiance, and backup generation for extended cloudy periods. In the UK, off-grid solar is a niche application; most solar tile installations are grid-connected.

Synonyms: Stand-alone system · Autonomous system

See also: On-Grid · Battery Storage

Battery Storage
Battery storage systems (home batteries) store surplus solar electricity generated during the day for use during the evening and overnight, significantly increasing self-consumption rates and reducing grid electricity import. Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) chemistry currently dominates the residential market (e.g. Tesla Powerwall, Sonnen, GivEnergy, Solis) due to its safety, long cycle life (3,000–6,000 cycles), and improving cost per kWh. A typical UK residential battery is 5–15 kWh usable capacity, costing £3,000–£8,000 installed. Adding battery storage to a 4 kWp solar roof can increase self-consumption from ~35% to ~60–70%, improving payback and reducing carbon footprint. Batteries can also provide grid services (smart charging, virtual power plants) and emergency backup power.

Synonyms: Home battery · Solar battery

See also: Self-Consumption

G99 / G98 Grid Connection
G99 and G98 are the Engineering Recommendation (ER) standards published by the Energy Networks Association (ENA) that govern the connection of small-scale generating units (including solar PV) to the low-voltage distribution network in Great Britain. G98 applies to single and three-phase installations up to 16 A per phase (approximately 3.7 kW single-phase, 11 kW three-phase) — these require only a pre-notification to the DNO before connection. G99 applies to larger installations (over 16 A per phase) and requires formal application to the DNO, which must be approved before connection. Most residential solar PV systems ≤3.68 kWp are G98 (pre-notification only); larger systems and BIPV solar tile arrays above 3.68 kWp require a G99 application to the local DNO.

See also: On-Grid System

Compliance

EPC (Energy Performance Certificate)
An Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) is a document rating a building's energy efficiency on a scale of A (most efficient) to G (least efficient), calculated using the Standard Assessment Procedure (SAP) for dwellings or the Simplified Building Energy Model (SBEM) for non-domestic buildings. EPCs are legally required when a building is sold, let, or constructed. Solar PV systems improve a building's SAP score and EPC rating by reducing its predicted carbon emissions and energy cost. In the UK, an EPC rating of C or above is increasingly required for rental properties (via Minimum Energy Efficiency Standards, MEES) and is a target under the government's net-zero trajectory. Adding a BIPV solar tile system like the eQube can contribute meaningfully to improving a property's EPC band, particularly when combined with improved insulation and a heat pump.

See also: Part L

Part L & Solar Compliance
Part L of the Building Regulations (Conservation of Fuel and Power) sets minimum energy efficiency standards for new and substantially renovated buildings in England. The 2021 revision of Part L (effective from June 2022 for new dwellings) tightened notional dwelling energy performance by approximately 31% compared to the 2013 standard, requiring new homes to have a fabric energy efficiency standard (FEES) of 52 kWh/m²/year or lower and a primary energy rate 31% below the equivalent notional dwelling. Solar PV is an important tool for meeting Part L's carbon emission rate target (CO₂ target, not energy target alone): the SAP calculation credits on-site renewable generation. The Future Homes Standard (expected post-2025) will further tighten Part L, likely requiring renewable energy or zero-carbon heat as standard.
