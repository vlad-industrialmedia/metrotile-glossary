Glossary → Roof Structure & Anatomy
This section defines the structural components and anatomical features of a pitched roof — from the ridge at the apex to the eaves at the foot. Understanding these terms is essential for specifying, designing and installing a Metrotile steel roofing system correctly. All Metrotile installation guides, technical data sheets and BBA certificates use this standard terminology.

Roof Planes & Edges

Ridge
The ridge is the horizontal apex of a pitched roof — the highest point where the two (or more) main roof planes meet. The ridge runs horizontally along the top of the roof and is capped with a ridge tile or ridge cap to weatherproof the junction. On a Metrotile installation, the ridge is finished using a profiled steel ridge cap matching the tile range, secured with screws to the ridge board; no mortar is required (dry-fix system). The ridge height above the wall plate determines the roof's overall pitch for a given span.

See also: Ridge tile · Ridge cap · Ridge board

Valley
A valley is the internal angle (V-shaped channel) formed where two roof slopes meet and descend towards each other, typically at the junction of a dormer, bay window or intersecting wing of a building. The valley is one of the most critical junctions on any roof from a weatherproofing perspective, as it must manage rainwater flowing from both adjacent slopes simultaneously. Metrotile valley installations use a GRP moulded valley lining channel that provides a smooth, non-porous channel for water flow, with tile courses cut to the valley line on both sides.

See also: GRP valley lining · Valley tile · Valley rafter

Hip
A hip is the external, sloping angle formed where two adjacent roof planes meet at the corner of a building. Unlike a gable, which is a vertical triangular wall, a hip slopes at the same angle as the main roof planes on both sides. Hipped roofs are structurally more stable in high-wind conditions than gabled roofs, as the sloping hip surface offers less resistance to lateral wind pressure. On a Metrotile installation, the hip is finished with a profiled hip cap system fixed to a hip board or hip iron.

See also: Hip tile · Hip finish · Hip rafter

Eaves
The eaves is the lowest edge of a pitched roof, where the roof covering overhangs the exterior wall below and rainwater discharges into the guttering. The eaves detail typically incorporates a fascia board (at the rafter ends), a soffit board (closing the underside of the overhang), and guttering brackets. On a Metrotile installation, the bottom tile course is termed the eaves course, and an over-fascia ventilator is typically installed to admit airflow beneath the underlay into the ventilated roof space. Proper eaves detailing is critical for compliance with Building Regulations (Part C: weather resistance) and good practice guidance in BS 5534.

See also: Eaves course · Over-fascia vent · Fascia board

Verge
The verge is the edge of a roof at a gable wall — the sloping edge that runs from the ridge down to the eaves alongside the vertical gable end wall. Unlike the eaves which runs horizontally, the verge slopes in parallel with the roof pitch. Tiles at the verge must be trimmed and finished with a verge trim or bargeboard to prevent wind-driven rain from penetrating beneath the tile edge. On a Metrotile installation, profiled steel verge trims mechanically fixed to the bargeboard provide a clean, maintenance-free finish without mortar.

See also: Verge tile · Verge detail · Bargeboard

Gable
A gable is the triangular section of an exterior wall between the two slopes of a pitched roof at the end of a building. A gable wall rises to a point at the ridge and is bounded by the verge of the roof on each side. A gable end is the wall at the end of a ridged roof; a gable roof has gable ends at both ends of the building (as opposed to a hipped roof, which has sloping hips instead of vertical gable ends). The verge of the roof sits at the junction between the gable wall and the roof slope.

See also: Gable roof · Verge

Abutment
An abutment is the junction where a roof slope meets a vertical surface — typically a chimney breast, parapet wall, party wall or the wall of an adjacent higher building. Abutments require careful waterproofing using step flashings, soakers and/or cover flashings to prevent water ingress where the tile field ends against the vertical face. On a Metrotile installation, abutment details use profiled steel abutment pieces integrated with code lead or code aluminium flashings in accordance with the manufacturer's installation guide.

See also: Step flashing · Soaker · Abutment tile

Structural Timbers

Rafter
A rafter is a sloping structural timber member that runs from the ridge board at the roof's apex down to the wall plate at the top of the external wall. Rafters carry the dead load of the roof covering (tiles, battens, underlay), plus imposed loads from snow and wind. In a traditional cut roof, rafters are cut to length on site; in a trussed rafter roof, they are factory-fabricated as part of a pre-engineered truss. Rafter centres (typically 400mm or 600mm) determine the required batten size and fixing specification. For Metrotile installation, the rafters must be structurally adequate to support the tile system including wind uplift loading as specified in the BBA certificate.

See also: Roof truss · Tiling batten · Dead load

Purlin
A purlin is a horizontal structural timber member that spans between the gable walls or supporting posts of a building, running parallel to the ridge and eaves at mid-rafter height. Purlins provide intermediate support to the rafters, preventing them from bowing under load over long spans. The purlin is a key element of a traditional cut roof; trussed rafter roofs typically do not use purlins. In re-roofing projects, purlins must be assessed for adequacy when the roof covering changes — Metrotile's lighter weight often means existing purlins remain structurally adequate without reinforcement.

See also: Rafter · Dead load

Collar tie
A collar tie (or collar beam) is a horizontal timber member connecting two opposing rafters at a point above the wall plate, typically at approximately one-third to one-half of the rafter height. The collar tie resists the tendency of the rafters to spread outward under load, acting in tension to tie the two rafters together. Collar ties also contribute to the structural stability of the roof in high-wind conditions. In exposed attic or loft conversion designs, collar ties may form part of the visible roof structure.
Wall plate
The wall plate is a horizontal timber member bedded on top of the external masonry wall at eaves level, to which the feet of the rafters are fixed (bird's-mouthed) and which distributes the roof load evenly into the wall below. The wall plate is typically 100×50mm or 75×50mm treated softwood and is secured to the wall with metal straps at regular intervals for wind resistance. In trussed rafter construction, the truss bottoms are nailed to the wall plate.
Ridge board
The ridge board is a horizontal timber member running along the ridge of a cut roof, to which the upper ends of the rafters are nailed or bolted. The ridge board does not carry structural load in the way a beam does — it acts as a fixing point and spacer to maintain the geometry of the roof. In a trussed rafter roof, there is no separate ridge board — the ridge is formed by the apex of each truss. The Metrotile dry-fix ridge cap is screwed directly into the ridge board.

See also: Ridge · Ridge cap

Roof truss
A roof truss is a pre-engineered, factory-manufactured structural frame for a pitched roof, typically made from light-gauge timber members connected with metal gang-nail plates. Trusses are delivered to site ready to install and span from external wall to external wall without intermediate support, eliminating the need for load-bearing internal walls under the roof. Modern trussed rafter roofs are significantly faster to erect than cut roofs and offer consistent structural performance verified by structural calculation. Metrotile tiles are compatible with standard trussed rafter construction at the manufacturers' standard rafter centres.

Synonyms: trussed rafter · roof frame · prefabricated roof frame

Ceiling joist
A ceiling joist is a horizontal structural timber member spanning between the wall plates at eaves level, forming the base of the roof structure and the structural support for the ceiling below. Ceiling joists resist the outward thrust of the rafters (acting as ties) and carry the dead load of the ceiling finish. In a roof with a habitable loft, the ceiling joists may be replaced or supplemented with deeper floor joists to carry greater imposed loads.
Hip rafter
The hip rafter is a structural rafter running diagonally from the ridge board (or ridge intersection) down to the corner of the wall plate at a hip junction. The hip rafter supports the ends of the jack rafters that fill the hip plane. Hip rafters run at 45° to the main rafters (on a square plan) and carry a concentration of roof load at the building corner. Hip caps and hip tiles are fixed to a hip board screwed to or alongside the hip rafter.

See also: Hip · Hip finish

Valley rafter
The valley rafter is the structural rafter running diagonally along the valley — the internal intersection of two roof planes. Like the hip rafter, the valley rafter supports jack rafters on both sides and carries roof loading at a vulnerable junction. The valley rafter provides the structural base to which the valley lining (GRP channel or lead-lined board) is fixed. The valley board — a flat timber fixed on top of or alongside the valley rafter — provides a flat surface for the valley lining to sit on.

See also: Valley · GRP valley lining

Battens, Deck & Underlay

Tiling batten
A tiling batten (or tile batten) is a horizontal timber strip fixed across the rafters at regular centres to provide the fixing line for roofing tiles. On a Metrotile installation, battens are typically 38×25mm or 50×25mm treated (preservative-impregnated) softwood, fixed with two 3.35mm diameter annular ring shank nails or screws per rafter crossing. Batten sizes and fixing specifications must conform to BS 5534 for the span, tile weight and wind loading zone. Metrotile's installation guide specifies exact batten sizes, fixings and spacing for each profile and rafter centre.

See also: Batten gauge · Counter-batten · BS 5534

Counter-batten
A counter-batten is a timber batten fixed over the underlay directly along the line of each rafter, creating a ventilated air gap between the underlay and the tiling battens above. Counter-battens are used in two-layer (counter-batten) roof construction to ensure cross-ventilation above a vapour-permeable underlay and to allow any moisture that penetrates the tile line to drain freely to the eaves rather than sitting on the underlay. Counter-battens are typically 38×25mm or 50×38mm treated softwood.
Batten gauge
Batten gauge is the centre-to-centre spacing between consecutive tiling battens, expressed in millimetres. The batten gauge determines how much of each tile is exposed to the weather (the gauge equals the tile's exposed gauge), and it must match the profile's designed headlap. Standard Metrotile batten gauges are 450mm (for most profiles) or 900mm (for larger-format profiles). Using an incorrect batten gauge reduces headlap and compromises the tile's weathertightness and BBA certification compliance.

Synonyms: batten spacing · tile gauge · course spacing

Sarking
Sarking refers either to a rigid timber boarding (sarking board) fixed directly to the top of the rafters beneath the underlay, or in Scotland, to the underlay membrane itself. Sarking boards (typically 19mm or 25mm thick tongue-and-groove or plain-edged boards) provide a firm base for fixing and additional structural rigidity, particularly on older roofs being re-covered. Sarking is common in Scotland and northern England, where exposed upland conditions require the additional protection of a boarded substrate beneath the tile system.
Deck
The deck (or roof deck) is the continuous, solid surface covering the rafters of a flat or low-pitch roof, typically formed from oriented strand board (OSB), plywood, or tongued-and-grooved timber boards. Unlike a pitched tile roof which uses individual battens, a deck provides a continuous substrate for the waterproofing layer or low-pitch covering. In a flat-to-pitch conversion project, the original flat roof deck may be retained as a structural base for a new sloping framework and Metrotile tile system.

See also: Flat-to-pitch conversion

Roofing felt
Roofing felt is a bituminous or polyester-based membrane laid over the rafters beneath the tiling battens to provide a secondary barrier against wind-driven rain and dust, and to ensure rainwater that penetrates the tile line is directed to the eaves. Traditional roofing felt (BS EN 13707 Type 1F) is non-breathable and requires ventilated air gaps above and below it. It has largely been superseded on new installations by vapour-permeable underlay (VPU), though it remains in use for overlay onto existing felt on re-roofing projects.

See also: VPU (vapour permeable underlay)

VPU — Vapour Permeable Underlay
A VPU (Vapour Permeable Underlay), also called a breathable underlay or breathable membrane, is a roof underlay membrane that allows water vapour to pass from the warm interior of the building through the membrane to the outside, while preventing liquid water (rain) from passing inward. VPU eliminates the requirement for a ventilated cold roof void (typically 50mm below and 50mm above the felt) mandated by BS 5250 for traditional bitumen felt, simplifying roof construction and improving thermal efficiency. All Metrotile installations are designed to be compatible with VPU. The Metrotile-branded VAPR Free Extra Breathable Roof Underlay is an approved VPU.

Synonyms: breathable underlay · breathable membrane · vapour open underlay

Eaves & Soffit Details

Fascia board
The fascia board is a vertical timber or uPVC board fixed to the ends of the rafters at the eaves, closing off the roof structure and providing a fixing surface for the guttering. The fascia also supports the outer edge of the soffit board. In a Metrotile installation, the over-fascia ventilator is fixed to the top of the fascia to admit air into the roof void. Fascias are typically 19–25mm timber or cellular uPVC, available in various profiles.

See also: Soffit · Over-fascia vent · Eaves

Soffit
The soffit is the horizontal board or panel that closes the underside of the eaves overhang between the fascia board and the external wall. Soffits are typically timber, uPVC or fibre cement, and may incorporate ventilation slots or dedicated soffit vents to admit air into the roof void (in traditional non-VPU roof construction). In a VPU roof installation, the over-fascia ventilator replaces soffit vents as the primary means of ventilation entry.
Bargeboard
A bargeboard (or vergeboard) is a vertical or near-vertical board fitted along the verge of a pitched roof, covering the end of the tiling battens and rafters and providing a finished face to the gable. Bargeboards are typically timber or uPVC and may be plain or decoratively profiled. In a Metrotile installation, the verge trim is mechanically fixed to the bargeboard and the first tile course, providing a weathertight verge detail without mortar.

See also: Verge · Verge tile

Eaves course
The eaves course is the bottom row of tiles on a pitched roof, positioned at the eaves. The eaves course is the first to be installed and sets the geometry for all tile courses above it. On a Metrotile installation, the eaves course sits on or immediately above the over-fascia ventilator, with the bottom tile edge at the designed overhang to direct water into the gutter. A tilting fillet or eaves carrier may be used to provide the correct tail angle for the first course.
Drip edge
A drip edge is a metal flashing profile fixed at the eaves or verge, designed to direct water away from the fascia board and soffit into the guttering, while preventing capillary action drawing water back up under the tile edge. On a Metrotile installation, the underlay is lapped over the drip edge at the eaves so that any water penetrating the tile line is directed into the gutter rather than running back up under the underlay.
Guttering
Guttering (or rainwater gutters) are horizontal channels fixed at the eaves to collect rainwater discharged from the roof covering and direct it to downpipes and the surface or surface water drainage system. Standard UK guttering is half-round or ogee-section uPVC or cast iron, fixed to the fascia board at a slight fall toward the downpipe. Gutter sizing must be adequate to handle the peak rainfall flow from the roof catchment area — steep pitch roofs with large areas generate high flow rates. Metrotile's technical guidance includes rainwater run-off calculation references for gutter sizing.
