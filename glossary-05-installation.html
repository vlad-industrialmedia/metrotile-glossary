Installation & Fixing Methods

This section covers 32 terms relating to the installation of Metrotile lightweight steel roofing tiles: batten layout and gauging, tile fixing specifications, valley and hip details, abutment and ridge flashing, dry-fix systems, and compliance with BS 5534. Installation to these standards is required to maintain the Metrotile 40-year guarantee and BBA certification.

Battens & Deck Preparation

Tiling Batten
Tiling battens are horizontal timber strips fixed across the rafters (or counter-battens) to which the roofing tiles are nailed. For Metrotile steel tiles, battens are typically 38 × 25 mm treated softwood (treated to Use Class 2 or 3 per BS 8417). They must be graded to BS 5534 — free from large knots, shakes and wane — and fixed to each rafter with two nails at each crossing point. Batten size may be increased to 50 × 25 mm for rafter spacings greater than 600 mm.

See also: Counter-batten · Batten gauge

→ Technical documentation

Counter-Batten
Counter-battens (also called vertical battens or back battens) run parallel to the rafters, over the top of the breathable underlay, and provide a ventilated air gap between the underlay and the tiling battens. Counter-battens are typically 38 × 25 mm or 50 × 25 mm treated softwood. They are required when a non-ventilating underlay is used, or when a ventilated batten zone is specified for thermal or moisture management. The gap they create allows air circulation under the tiles and provides a path for any wind-driven moisture to drain to the eaves.

Synonyms: Vertical batten · Back batten

Batten Gauge
Batten gauge is the centre-to-centre spacing of horizontal tiling battens, measured along the rafter. It directly determines the exposed face (margin) of each tile course. For Metrotile profiles the gauge is specified in the product data sheet and BBA certificate and typically ranges from 300 mm to 370 mm depending on the profile and pitch. The gauge must be calculated from the ridge down to the eaves so that the courses work out evenly, with the top (ridge) course and bottom (eaves) course cut or adjusted to suit.

Synonyms: Batten spacing · Tile margin · Tile course spacing

Batten Spacing
Batten spacing is sometimes used interchangeably with batten gauge, but can also refer to the horizontal spacing between battens at the verge — i.e., the distance from the last batten to the verge board. Correct lateral spacing ensures that the verge tile overhangs the barge board or verge trim by the specified amount (typically 50–75 mm) to throw water clear of the fascia.

See also: Batten gauge · Verge detail

Eaves Batten
The eaves batten is the lowest tiling batten, positioned at the eaves to receive the bottom course of tiles. For Metrotile, the eaves batten position is set so that the bottom edge of the first tile course overhangs the fascia board by the specified amount (typically 50–65 mm) to direct rainwater into the gutter. The eaves batten is often set on a tilting fillet (eaves tilting fillet) to bring the first course of tiles up to the correct angle relative to subsequent courses.

See also: Eaves detail · Over-fascia vent

Ridge Batten
The ridge batten is a horizontal batten or board fixed along the apex of the roof to which the ridge caps are fixed in a dry-fix system. In Metrotile installations, the ridge batten receives the mechanical fixings for the ridge cap tiles and may also be used to anchor the top edge of the breathable underlay. Ridge battens must be fixed securely to both rafters or to a structural ridge board, as they carry the wind uplift loads transmitted by the ridge caps.

See also: Ridge cap · Dry-fix

Sarking Board
Sarking is a continuous layer of boards or sheet material fixed directly to the rafters beneath the battens, forming a rigid sarking deck. In Scotland and northern England, sarking boards (typically 25 mm treated softwood boards) are traditional and provide structural rigidity, additional thermal resistance and a substrate for the underlay. In most of England and Wales, sarking is not standard — the underlay is draped directly between the rafters. Sarking improves the racking resistance of the roof and provides a secondary line of weather defence.

See also: Deck · Breathable underlay

Tile Fixing & Nailing

Clout Nail
Clout nails are large-headed, short-shank nails used for fixing roofing tiles and underlay. The large flat head distributes the fixing load over the tile surface and prevents the nail head from pulling through. For Metrotile steel tiles, clout nails must be hot-dip galvanised steel, 45 mm × 3.35 mm, with an annular ring shank for enhanced withdrawal resistance. Two nails are used per tile in most exposure zones. All nails must penetrate fully through the tile and into the batten with a minimum 25 mm batten penetration.

Synonyms: Roofing nail · Tack (informal) · Screw shank nail

Annular Ring Shank Nail
Annular ring shank nails (also called ring shank nails or screw shank nails) have a series of circumferential rings along the shank that grip the timber fibres as they are driven in. The rings dramatically increase withdrawal resistance compared to smooth shank nails — typically by 150–200% — making them the specified fastener for roofing tiles under BS 5534. Metrotile specifies hot-dip galvanised annular ring shank clout nails for all tile fixings to ensure long-term corrosion resistance matching the 40-year tile guarantee.

Synonyms: Ring shank nail · Screw shank nail

Cross Nail
Cross nailing is a supplementary nailing method where a second nail is driven through the tile at an angle across the first nail to create a cross pattern in the batten. It is used in very high exposure zones where additional uplift resistance is required. Metrotile BBA certificate 07/4470 specifies the nailing pattern (including cross nailing or additional clips) for each wind exposure zone. Cross nailing is additional to, not a replacement for, standard two-nail fixing.
VPU Clip
VPU clips (Verge, Pitch, Universal) are mechanical fixing clips that attach to the underside of a Metrotile tile and clip onto the tiling batten, providing a secondary fixing without requiring an additional nail hole in the tile face. They are used at the verge (where the tile cannot be nailed through its normal nail position), at the ridge course (where tiles are cut and lose their nail hole), and in high-exposure zones where BS 5534 requires additional uplift resistance. VPU clips are manufactured from stainless steel or hot-dip galvanised steel.

See also: Verge detail · Fixing specification

Batten Screw
Batten screws are used in addition to nails in very high exposure zones, or as a primary fixing where the batten must be anchored with greater force than nails can provide. For Metrotile ridge caps and hip caps, self-drilling stainless steel or hot-dip galvanised screws are used to fix the cap tiles to the ridge batten in the dry-fix system. Screws must not over-drive through the tile — a screw gun with torque control or a depth-stop collar is used to ensure the screw compresses the tile without cracking it.
Fixing Specification
Fixing specification is the engineering document — derived from BS 5534 and the BBA certificate — that prescribes the exact number, type, size and position of fixings for each tile at each roof position (field, verge, ridge, eaves, etc.) for a given exposure zone and pitch. Metrotile provides a site-specific fixing specification as part of its technical support service. Using the correct fixing specification is a condition of the 40-year guarantee and BBA certification.

See also: BS 5534 · BBA Certificate

Ridge, Hip & Dry-Fix

Dry-Fix
Dry-fix is the system of mechanically fixing ridge, hip and verge tiles using clips, screws and compressible seals instead of traditional sand:cement mortar. Following BS 5534:2014+A2:2018, mortar bedding alone is no longer acceptable for mechanically fixing ridge and hip tiles on new installations — all must be mechanically fixed. Dry-fix systems eliminate the cracking, freeze-thaw deterioration and maintenance problems associated with mortar. Metrotile's ridge cap system is designed as a fully dry-fix solution, using a foam eaves filler strip and self-drilling fixings into a ridge batten.

Contrast: Mortar bedding

Ridge Cap
Ridge caps (also called ridge tiles) are purpose-designed capping tiles that cover the apex of the roof where two slopes meet at the ridge. Metrotile ridge caps are roll-formed from the same ZAM®-coated steel as the field tiles, finished with the same stone chip surface for a consistent appearance. They are supplied in half-round and angular profiles to suit different tile profiles. Ridge caps are fixed dry using mechanical screws into a ridge batten and sealed at both ends with a compressible eaves filler and end stops.

Synonyms: Ridge tile · Ridge capping

→ Ridge cap accessories

Hip Cap
Hip caps are purpose-designed capping tiles that cover the hip intersection — where two slopes meet at the sloping hip rafter. Like ridge caps, Metrotile hip caps are roll-formed steel with a stone chip finish. They are fixed dry to a hip board or hip rafter and overlap downward from ridge to eaves in the same manner as field tiles. The bottom of each hip cap is cut at the eaves to align with the tile coursing. At the junction with the ridge, hip caps are mitred and sealed with a hip-ridge junction piece.

Synonyms: Hip tile · Hip capping

Ridge Board
The ridge board (or ridge beam) is the horizontal structural timber running along the apex of the roof between the gable ends, into which the upper ends of the rafters are nailed. It is distinct from the ridge batten, which is a non-structural fixing batten for ridge caps. In a traditionally framed roof, the ridge board is typically 25–32 mm thick by 150–225 mm deep. In a trussed rafter roof, there is usually no structural ridge board — the truss geometry provides the ridge geometry without one.

See also: Rafter · Ridge batten

Eaves Filler
Eaves fillers (also called eaves ventilation fillers or profile fillers) are shaped compressible foam strips that fill the corrugated gap at the eaves between the bottom edge of the tile and the eaves batten or fascia. They prevent birds, bats, insects and wind-driven debris from entering the batten void while maintaining a ventilation opening for air to enter the batten zone. For ridge and hip caps, a continuous compressible foam filler is used to seal the underside of the cap along its length. Metrotile supplies profile-specific eaves fillers for each tile profile.

Synonyms: Profile filler · Ventilator strip · Under-tile vent

Mortar Bedding
Mortar bedding is the traditional method of bedding and pointing ridge and hip tiles in sand:cement mortar (typically a 3:1 or 4:1 mix by volume). Mortar bedding was the standard method before dry-fix systems existed, but it is susceptible to cracking due to differential thermal movement between the tile and mortar, and can fail due to freeze-thaw cycling in exposed climates. Since BS 5534:2014+A2:2018 required mechanical fixing of all ridges and hips, mortar alone is no longer accepted for new installations. Mortar may still be used as an additional sealant under a dry-fixed cap but cannot be the primary fixing.

Contrast: Dry-fix

Valley & Abutment Details

Valley Installation
Valley installation refers to the process of waterproofing and tiling the valley — the internal angle where two roof slopes meet. Valleys are the most vulnerable part of a tiled roof to water ingress because they concentrate the run-off from two slopes. Valley installation requires careful undercloaking, lining and cutting of the adjacent tiles. Metrotile valleys are typically formed using GRP (glass-reinforced polyester) valley linings or lead-effect valley linings, with field tiles cut at an angle and lapped over the valley lining edges.

See also: GRP valley lining · Open valley

Open Valley
An open valley is a valley in which a visible channel of waterproofing material runs down the centre of the valley, with the adjacent tiles cut back so a strip of lining material is exposed. Open valleys allow debris to wash freely out of the valley and are easier to clean and maintain than closed valleys. The visible lining is typically GRP, lead, aluminium or a lead-effect pressed steel valley tray. Metrotile GRP valley linings are designed for open valley installation.

Contrast: Closed valley

Closed Valley
A closed valley (also called a swept or laced valley) is formed by weaving or cutting field tiles across the valley line so that no lining is visible — the tiles themselves cover the valley. Closed valleys give a more traditional appearance and are common on clay and slate roofs, but are more complex to construct and require smaller tiles near the apex. Closed valleys are not standard practice with Metrotile steel tiles; the preferred method is an open valley with a GRP or lead-effect lining.

Synonyms: Swept valley · Laced valley · Woven valley

GRP Valley Lining
GRP valley linings (glass-reinforced polyester, also called fibreglass valley linings) are pre-formed U-channel trays moulded to the valley profile of the roof. They are lightweight, rot-proof, maintenance-free and available in lengths up to 3 m. Metrotile supplies GRP valley linings in a dark grey (lead-like) finish specifically profiled to complement Metrotile tile coursing. GRP valley linings are lapped with tiles cut at the valley angle, and the side edges are clipped or tack-nailed to the valley board to prevent uplift.

Synonyms: Fibreglass valley · GRP valley tray

Abutment Flashing
Abutment flashings seal the junction between the roof slope and a vertical or near-vertical wall surface — typically a chimney, parapet, gable wall or the wall of an adjacent taller building. The flashing must be weather-tight, allow differential movement between the roof structure and the wall, and be durable for the life of the roof. Lead flashing (Code 3–4, to BS EN 12588) is the traditional standard and is suitable for all Metrotile applications. Aluminium or lead-free alternatives are used in coastal zones where the salt air attacks lead over time.

See also: Step flashing · Soaker

Step Flashing
Step flashing is a series of L-shaped metal pieces (steps) that dress over each course of tiles and up the adjacent wall, overlapping each other as they follow the tile courses up the slope. Step flashing is used at abutments where the roof meets a brick or masonry wall: each step piece is tucked under the tile above and behind a mortar joint in the wall above, sealing the junction course by course. Step flashing is typically cut from Code 3 lead sheet and counterflashed with a cover flashing pressed into a horizontal mortar joint above.

See also: Abutment flashing · Soaker

Soaker
Soakers are small L-shaped metal pieces placed under each tile course at an abutment, between the tiles and the wall, to direct any water that penetrates behind the step flashing away from the roof timbers. Soakers work in conjunction with step flashing: the soaker lies flat on the tile below and turns up the wall; the step flashing overlaps the soaker and diverts water outward. Soakers are typically 175 mm × (tile gauge + 25 mm) and cut from Code 3 lead.

See also: Step flashing

Verge & Eaves Details

Verge Detail
The verge is the edge of the roof at a gable end — where the roof slope meets the gable wall or barge board. Verge detailing must prevent wind-driven rain from entering under the tiles at the open edge and must secure the exposed tile edge against wind uplift. Metrotile verge details use a purpose-made verge trim fixed to the barge board, with the last tile course secured using VPU clips and/or nailed through the verge trim. The tile overhang at the verge is typically 50 mm beyond the barge board face.

See also: VPU clip · Verge (structural)

Verge Tile
Verge tiles are the tiles at the verge edge — the last tile in each course before the gable end. They are typically standard field tiles cut or adjusted to suit the verge overhang, and fixed with VPU clips in addition to (or instead of) nails. Some profiles have a dedicated left-hand and right-hand verge tile with a pre-formed drip edge to direct water off the gable wall. Correct verge tile fixing is critical to the wind performance of the roof in exposed locations.
Eaves Detail
Eaves detail refers to the construction of the lowest edge of the roof — where the tiles overhang the fascia and discharge into the guttering. Metrotile eaves detail includes: the eaves batten position, a tilting fillet to set the first tile course at the correct angle, an eaves filler strip to seal the under-tile gap against bird and insect ingress, the over-fascia ventilator (OFV) for batten zone ventilation, and the tile overhang into the gutter (typically 50 mm past the fascia face). The eaves is also where the breathable underlay is dressed down into the gutter.

See also: Over-fascia vent · Eaves batten

Over-Fascia Vent (OFV)
An over-fascia vent (OFV) is a ventilation strip fixed to the top of the fascia board, providing a continuous air inlet at the eaves for the batten ventilation zone. It incorporates a mesh to exclude birds and insects while allowing free airflow. OFVs are required when the breathable underlay is draped into the gutter (which seals the eaves air gap) or when counter-battens are used to create a ventilated batten zone. Metrotile supplies profiled OFVs to match their tile coursing, ensuring adequate ventilation area (typically ≥10 mm equivalent air path per BS 5250).

Synonyms: Eaves ventilator · Soffit vent (different position — soffit vents are in the soffit board, OFVs are on top of the fascia)

Drip Edge
A drip edge is a shaped metal strip (typically aluminium or galvanised steel) fixed to the eaves or verge to form a neat edge and direct water into the gutter or away from the wall face. At the eaves, a drip edge or eaves trim is used to support the leading edge of the breathable underlay and form a clean fascia-to-gutter junction. At the verge, a drip edge trim is used to form the edge of the last tile course and give a weathered profile over the barge board.
Junction Piece
Junction pieces are purpose-made pressed steel accessories that cover the intersection of ridge caps with hip caps (hip-ridge junction), or the Y-shaped intersection where three roof slopes meet (Y-junction) or five slopes meet (five-way junction). These junctions arise in complex roof geometries such as multiple hips, Dutch gables and T-shaped ridge plans. Metrotile supplies pressed steel junction pieces in matching stone chip finish for all standard ridge and hip configurations.

See also: Ridge cap · Hip cap
