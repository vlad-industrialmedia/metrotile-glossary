Project Types

New Build
New build refers to the construction of a building on a previously undeveloped or cleared site, as opposed to conversion or extension of an existing structure. On new-build projects, the roof structure is designed to carry the specified cladding load from the outset, so there are no overloading concerns with any compliant tile type. Lightweight steel tiles bring particular advantages on new build: faster roof-on times accelerate the programme, reduced dead load may allow slimmer roof structure, and the tiles' long warranty (40 years) provides confidence for developers and buyers. New-build warranties such as NHBC Buildmark cover the structure; the Metrotile tile warranty is a separate product guarantee.

Refurbishment
Refurbishment is the repair, renovation, or upgrading of an existing building to improve its condition, performance, or appearance — without fundamentally changing its use or structure. In roofing, refurbishment typically means replacing an end-of-life covering (worn concrete tiles, leaking flat roof) with a new system. Lightweight steel tiles are particularly advantageous in refurbishment: their low weight (~7–8 kg/m²) often falls within the load capacity of aging timber roof structures without reinforcement, while their installation speed reduces scaffold and programme costs. Refurbishment also offers the opportunity to upgrade insulation, membranes, and ventilation to current Building Regulation standards.

Retrofit
Retrofit in the construction industry means upgrading an existing building's fabric, systems, or energy performance — typically improving insulation, airtightness, heating systems, or renewables — to reduce its carbon footprint and running costs. The UK government's Heat and Buildings Strategy (2021) targets the retrofit of 29 million homes to EPC Band C by 2035. Re-roofing is often the trigger event that enables whole-roof retrofit: replacing an old covering creates the opportunity to upgrade roof insulation (e.g., from 100 mm mineral wool to 200 mm PIR), install a new breathable membrane, improve eaves ventilation, and potentially add integrated solar tiles. Metrotile's lightweight system is compatible with all these retrofit measures.

See also: EnerPHit · Flat-to-Pitch Conversion

Re-roofing
Re-roofing is the complete replacement of a roof's weatherproof covering, typically including the underlay, battens, and tile or sheet, while retaining the existing roof structure. It is the most common roofing project type in the UK, driven by the aging housing stock (many properties still have concrete tiles laid in the 1970s–90s approaching end of life). Metrotile steel tiles are a popular re-roofing choice because: the low weight means the existing structure is rarely overstressed; the profile range replicates the look of the original covering; the 40-year warranty gives homeowners long-term peace of mind; and installation speed minimises exposure of the interior to weather.

See also: Overlay Roofing · Refurbishment

Overlay Roofing
Overlay roofing (also called over-roofing or re-covering) is the installation of a new tile system directly over the existing roof covering, without stripping the old material first. It is economically attractive because it eliminates the cost of stripping, skip hire, and disposal — and significantly reduces programme time and disruption. The low weight of Metrotile tiles (~7–8 kg/m² installed) makes overlay feasible on most existing structures that can accept an additional 10 kg/m² or so without exceeding design load. The existing covering must be structurally sound, reasonably flat, and not actively failing; a structural appraisal is recommended before overlay installation. BS 5534 principles apply to the new tile installation, including batten sizing and fixing specification.

Synonyms: Over-roofing · Re-covering

See also: Re-roofing · Social Housing

Flat-to-Pitch Conversion
Flat-to-pitch conversion is the replacement of a flat roof with a new pitched roof structure, typically using a lightweight A-frame or trussed rafter system erected over the existing flat deck. It is popular in social housing and 1960s–70s housing stock where ageing built-up felt or asphalt flat roofs have reached the end of their life and are expensive to maintain. Metrotile is the preferred tile for many flat-to-pitch projects because the tiles weigh just 7–8 kg/m² — far below the structural capacity of the lightweight A-frame — and their minimum 10° pitch suits the low-profile A-frames used where head height is limited. The conversion also adds loft space and improves the building's thermal performance.

Loft Conversion
A loft conversion transforms an existing unused roof space into habitable accommodation, typically adding one or more bedrooms. Common types include roof light (Velux) conversions (minimal structural change), dormer conversions (box dormers or L-shaped dormers extending roof volume), hip-to-gable conversions (extending the hip end to create a full gable), and mansard conversions (reshaping the entire roof to maximise internal height). Where a loft conversion involves re-roofing or new dormer cladding, lightweight steel tiles are advantageous: they impose minimal additional load on the modified structure, come in profiles that match the existing roof covering, and can be detailed to meet the thermal and acoustic performance required for habitable rooms. Building Regulations Part A (Structure), Part B (Fire), Part E (Sound) and Part L (Energy) all apply.

Residential

Social Housing
Social housing encompasses affordable rented and shared-ownership homes managed by local authorities, housing associations (registered providers), and ALMOs (Arms Length Management Organisations). The sector owns approximately 4 million homes in England, many from post-war building programmes with aging concrete tile or flat roofs requiring replacement. Social housing programmes typically procure roofing via framework agreements (e.g., Procurement for Housing, Fusion21, NHS Shared Business Services) and require BBA-certified products, competitive pricing, programme certainty, and whole-life cost evidence. Metrotile's lightweight tiles, 40-year guarantee, and track record across hundreds of social housing schemes make them a default specification in many local authority and housing association procurement frameworks.

See also: Flat-to-Pitch · Overlay Roofing

Self-Build
Self-build is a procurement route where an individual commissions or directly manages the construction of their own home — either building themselves (true self-build) or employing a contractor (custom build). Self-build accounts for approximately 7–10% of new homes in the UK annually. For self-builders, lightweight steel tiles offer ease of handling (each tile is light enough for one person to carry), a straightforward mechanical fixing system, a design-life guarantee, and a wide profile and colour range to satisfy planning conditions. BBA certification provides the warranty insurance (structural warranty lenders) with product assurance. The Self-Build and Custom Housebuilding Act 2015 gives self-builders rights to plots via local authority registers.

See also: New Build · Kit Home

Modular Home
A modular home is a factory-manufactured building assembled from pre-fabricated volumetric units (modules) transported to site and craned into position. The roof of a modular home may be pre-tiled in the factory (if profile allows crane-lifting without damage) or tiled on site after module erection. Lightweight steel tiles are particularly well-suited to modular construction: their low weight minimises crane capacity requirements; their mechanical fixing is compatible with both timber and light steel framing (LSF) used in modular construction; and factory tiling is feasible because individual tiles are light and robust. The growing offsite construction sector has driven increasing specification of Metrotile on modular housing, schools, and healthcare schemes.

Synonyms: Volumetric modular · Factory-built home

See also: Kit Home

Kit Home
A kit home (or package home) is a self-build or developer-supplied house where the structural frame, panels, and often the cladding system are manufactured off-site as a kit and erected on a prepared foundation. Kit homes include timber frame, structural insulated panels (SIPS), and light steel framing (LSF) systems. Metrotile is frequently specified as the roofing system for kit homes because it is supplied as a complete kit of tiles, battens, ridge, hip, and verge components with a single-source guarantee — complementing the kit-supply model of the structural frame. The lightweight tiles also allow slimmer truss sections than heavy tiles, reducing structural material costs.

See also: Modular Home · Self-Build

Single-Storey Extension
A single-storey extension is a ground-floor addition to an existing residential or commercial building, typically built under Permitted Development rights (subject to size limits) or with full planning permission. The roof of an extension must be carefully designed to integrate visually and structurally with the host building. Lightweight steel tiles are popular for extensions because they can match the profile and colour of the main roof exactly (important for planning); their low pitch capability (10°+) suits the shallow lean-to or monopitch roofs common on rear extensions; and their light weight does not impose excessive loads on the extension's wall structure.

See also: Outbuilding · Conservation Area

Outbuilding & Garden Room
Outbuildings and garden rooms include garages, garden offices, studios, summer houses, ancillary accommodation, and leisure buildings in the curtilage of a dwelling. Many smaller outbuildings are built under Permitted Development without planning permission; larger or permanent structures require consent. For outbuilding roofs, lightweight steel tiles provide a traditional pitched-roof appearance at relatively low cost, with minimal structural requirements and a long service life. Garden rooms used as home offices fall under Building Regulation Part L (energy) and may need insulated roofs to habitable room standards. Metrotile's range includes profiles suitable for small-span mono-pitch outbuilding roofs from 10° pitch upwards.

See also: Garage & Carport · Extension

Garage & Carport
Garages and carports are common ancillary structures requiring lightweight, durable, low-maintenance roofing. Integral garages (attached to or beneath the main house) are subject to Building Regulations; detached garages are generally exempt if under 30 m². For domestic garages, Metrotile's low minimum pitch (10°) suits standard shallow-pitched garage roof structures, and the tiles are available in profiles and colours that match or complement the house roof. The Metrotile Slate 900 Anti-Vandal tile (a smooth flat-profile steel tile) is particularly popular for garages and car parks where a low-profile, graffiti-resistant surface is required.

See also: Anti-Vandal Application

Commercial & Specialist

Commercial Building
Commercial buildings encompass offices, retail units, restaurants, hotels, leisure facilities, healthcare, and other non-residential non-industrial buildings. Commercial pitched roofing typically requires BBA or ETA certification for product assurance, compliance with Part L (energy) of the Building Regulations (which has more stringent U-value and airtightness targets for commercial buildings), and increasingly BREEAM assessment. Lightweight steel tiles are specified on commercial buildings where an aesthetically traditional roof form is required — particularly on mixed-use developments, hotels, retail parks, and healthcare facilities. The tiles' low weight is valuable where a commercial building's steel or concrete frame needs to minimise cladding dead load.

See also: Mixed-Use · BREEAM

Industrial Building
Industrial buildings include factories, warehouses, distribution centres, workshops, and agricultural buildings. Many industrial pitched roofs use cold-rolled steel sheeting (trapezoidal or sinusoidal profile), but where a building's location requires a more traditional aesthetic — a rural setting, a conservation area, or a high-quality business park — flat-profile steel tile systems provide a residential appearance at commercial scale. Metrotile tiles have been used on enterprise centres, farm diversification buildings, and rural industrial units where planning authorities require a roof that blends with the agricultural landscape. Load-bearing capacity and span are determined by the structural purlins rather than the tiles themselves.

See also: Rural & Agricultural

Schools & Education Buildings
Schools and education buildings present specific roofing challenges: large roof areas, high public visibility, strict acoustic and fire performance requirements (Part E and Part B), BIM mandates on publicly funded projects, and the need for minimal maintenance during term time. Lightweight steel tiles score well against these criteria: their light weight suits the modular construction methods now common in school buildings (DfE's Priority School Building Programme has used Metrotile on multiple schemes); their acoustic performance reduces rain noise impact; and their fire classification (Class A or B to BS EN 13501-5) satisfies typical specification requirements. BREEAM Education assessment is common on publicly funded school projects.

See also: Modular Construction · BREEAM

Holiday Lodge & Leisure Building
Holiday lodges, leisure buildings, and holiday parks are a specialist market segment where attractive, low-maintenance roofing is commercially important. Holiday park operators and lodge manufacturers require roofing that: looks traditional and attractive to holiday guests; requires no maintenance between annual park closures; withstands coastal and exposed rural sites; and has a service life aligned with the lodge's chassis warranty. Metrotile's profiles — particularly Shingle and Woodshake — are popular on luxury lodges and holiday park buildings precisely because they combine visual appeal with negligible maintenance requirements. The tiles' wind resistance in high wind zones is an additional benefit for exposed coastal and upland sites.

See also: Coastal Location

Mixed-Use Development
Mixed-use development combines residential and non-residential uses (retail, office, leisure) within a single building or masterplan. Mixed-use buildings often require a consistent architectural language across the roof — one system visible from apartments above and street level below. Lightweight steel tiles can be specified across the entire roof to provide visual consistency while accommodating different structural approaches (timber frame on residential upper floors, steel frame on retail ground floor) without overloading any element. BIM coordination across disciplines is standard practice on mixed-use schemes; Metrotile's NBS Plus specification clauses and BIM objects support digital coordination.

See also: Commercial Building · LPA

Anti-Vandal Application
Anti-vandal roofing applications require tiles that resist graffiti, are difficult to remove without specialist tools, and have no surface features that provide hand or foot holds for climbing. The Metrotile Slate 900 Anti-Vandal tile is specifically designed for these applications: a smooth flat-profile interlocking steel tile with concealed mechanical fixings that cannot be removed without removing the tile itself. It is widely used on prison and custody suites, mental health secure units, school bicycle shelters, car parks, and public transport infrastructure. The smooth surface can be cleaned of paint graffiti without damage to the tile. The tile meets Secured by Design principles and satisfies Local Authority security requirements.

Context & Planning

Rural & Agricultural Building
Rural and agricultural buildings — including barns, farm buildings, stables, and rural enterprise units — are often located in areas of outstanding natural beauty (AONB), national parks, or green belt where planning policy requires roofing materials to respect the local character. Lightweight steel tiles in natural-tone stone chip finishes (slate grey, terracotta, moss green) are increasingly accepted by planning authorities as a modern equivalent to clay, slate, or natural stone tiles on rural buildings. Their durability in harsh exposed environments (freeze-thaw, high UV, driving rain and wind) and minimal maintenance requirements suit agricultural contexts where scaffolding is difficult and disruptive. Farm diversification projects (holiday lets, glamping pods, offices) frequently specify Metrotile.

See also: Industrial Building · Conservation Area

Coastal & Exposed Location
Coastal and exposed locations subject roofing systems to elevated wind loads, marine salt spray, freeze-thaw cycling, and high UV levels. In BS EN 1991-1-4 terms, coastal and upland sites are typically Wind Exposure Category D (severe), with peak gust speeds up to 56 m/s or above. Metrotile's ZAM® coating (zinc-aluminium-magnesium alloy) provides excellent resistance to salt-laden air and corrosion in coastal environments — outperforming standard galvanising and PVDF by up to three times in accelerated salt-spray testing. All Metrotile products are tested to the wind resistance requirements of BS 5534 and EN 14437, making them suitable for the UK's most exposed coastal sites, from Cornwall to the Orkneys.

Heritage & Listed Building
Heritage buildings and listed buildings are protected by the Planning (Listed Buildings and Conservation Areas) Act 1990. Listed buildings are classified Grade I (exceptional interest), Grade II* (particularly important), or Grade II (nationally important). Any works that affect the character of a listed building — including re-roofing — require Listed Building Consent (LBC) from the LPA, in addition to any planning permission. Historic England's guidance advises that the choice of roofing material should match or be sympathetic to the original material. While Metrotile tiles are unlikely to be approved as a direct replacement for natural Welsh slate on a Grade I farmhouse, they may be considered for outbuildings, later extensions, or lower-heritage-value structures where whole-life cost and structural load are overriding factors.

See also: Conservation Area · LPA

Conservation Area
A conservation area is a designated area of special architectural or historic interest, the character or appearance of which it is desirable to preserve or enhance, as defined under the Planning (Listed Buildings and Conservation Areas) Act 1990. There are approximately 10,000 conservation areas in England. Within them, planning permission is required for works that would not normally need it — including demolition of certain structures and, in some areas, changes to roof materials that alter the character of the area. Metrotile profiles designed to replicate slate (iPanel Slate), clay Roman, or natural stone profiles have been accepted by some LPAs in conservation area settings, subject to pre-application discussions and agreement of samples. Metrotile's technical team can supply profile samples and precedent evidence to support planning applications.

See also: Listed Building · LPA

Local Planning Authority (LPA)
The Local Planning Authority (LPA) is the council or other body responsible for administering the planning system in a given area — determining planning applications, enforcing planning conditions, and preparing Local Development Plans. In England, LPAs are typically district, borough, city, or county councils. For roofing projects, the LPA may require planning permission where: the property is a listed building; it is in a conservation area; the project involves a change of material that would materially affect the external appearance; or permitted development rights have been removed by a condition on a previous consent. Early pre-application dialogue with the LPA's planning officer is advisable on any sensitive project, and Metrotile's team can supply technical information, profile samples, and precedent examples to support the application.

Synonyms: Planning authority · Planning office
