Conservatory Roofing

This section defines 28 terms relating to conservatory roofing — covering conservatory styles and structures, solid roof conversions, planning and Building Regulations requirements, thermal performance, and the specific challenges of converting polycarbonate or glass roofs to insulated solid tiled roofs using Metrotile lightweight steel tiles.

Conservatory Styles

Lean-To Conservatory
A lean-to conservatory is the simplest conservatory form — a single sloping roof abutting the rear or side of the house wall. The roof is a mono-pitch, typically at 10–25°, and the structure is usually lower than other conservatory styles. Lean-tos are popular because they are simple to construct and fit easily under first-floor windows. The mono-pitch roof is well suited to Metrotile's low-pitch profiles (Aquapan, Cambridge Xtreme) for solid roof conversion. The abutment detail between the new tiled roof and the house wall is critical and typically uses lead or aluminium step flashing.

Synonyms: Mono-pitch conservatory · Simple conservatory

Victorian Conservatory
A Victorian conservatory has a distinctive multi-faceted hipped roof — typically a five-faceted apex (pentagonal plan) or seven-faceted (heptagonal) plan, with a steeply pitched multi-hip roof form and a decorative ridge finial. Victorian conservatories are the most commonly sold conservatory style in the UK and have the most complex roof geometry, with multiple hips meeting at the apex. Metrotile solid roof conversions on Victorian conservatories require careful hip cap detailing and junction pieces at the complex multi-hip apex.

See also: Hip roof · Junction piece

Edwardian Conservatory
An Edwardian conservatory has a square or rectangular plan with a simple hipped or gable roof — typically at 25–35° pitch. The Edwardian style provides more usable floor area and a simpler roof geometry than the Victorian style. The gable-fronted Edwardian conservatory is particularly well suited to solid roof conversion with Metrotile, as the gable roof is straightforward to batten, tile and flash. The large glazed gable end panel is retained in glass or replaced with rendered brickwork in full conversion projects.
P-Shaped Conservatory
A P-shaped conservatory combines a lean-to section with a Victorian or Edwardian bay, creating a P or L plan when viewed from above. The roof is therefore a combination of two different roof forms joined at a valley. P-shaped conservatories are popular for larger plots and provide both a dining area (lean-to section) and a sitting area (bay section). The valley junction between the two roof forms is the critical waterproofing detail in a solid roof conversion.

See also: T-shaped conservatory

T-Shaped Conservatory
A T-shaped conservatory is a similar compound form to the P-shaped, with a central lean-to wing and a bay projecting forward at right angles to form a T plan. Like P-shaped conservatories, T-shaped structures have valley intersections between the two roof sections. Both P and T-shaped conservatories require careful structural assessment before solid roof conversion to confirm that the frame can carry the combined loads of the new insulated roof.
Orangery
An orangery is a substantial glazed garden room built to higher quality standards than a standard conservatory — typically with solid masonry or brick walls, a flat or low-pitch glazed lantern roof over a larger solid flat-roof perimeter, and architectural detailing in keeping with the house. Orangeries are often subject to full Building Regulations as extensions rather than being permitted as conservatories. The perimeter roof of an orangery — where solid meets glazed lantern — is often tiled with Metrotile to create a weathertight weathering zone.

See also: Lantern roof

Lantern Roof
A lantern roof is a raised central roof element over a flat or low-pitch roof, glazed on all four sides to admit daylight from above. Lantern roofs are characteristic of orangeries and kitchen extensions. The lantern typically sits on a timber or aluminium frame, with its perimeter sealed to the flat or low-pitch roof below. The surrounding flat roof areas — the perimeter "walkway" around the lantern — may be tiled with Metrotile for a more robust and attractive weathering layer than felt or EPDM.

Conservatory Structure

Dwarf Wall
A dwarf wall is a low masonry wall, typically 450–600 mm high, forming the base perimeter of a conservatory below the glazed frame. Dwarf walls provide a solid base for the conservatory frame, prevent ground damp from entering, improve security and reduce draughts at the bottom of the glazing. In solid roof conversions, dwarf walls must be adequate to carry the additional load of the new insulated roof. The structural adequacy of the dwarf walls, their foundations and the conservatory frame is assessed before any solid roof conversion.
Conservatory Frame
The conservatory frame is the aluminium, UPVC or timber structural framework that carries the glazed panels and the roof. In a standard solid roof conversion, the existing frame is retained and the new roof structure is built on top of it — replacing only the roof panels. The frame must be in sound condition and capable of carrying the additional dead load of the new insulated solid roof system. Metrotile solid roof systems typically add 15–25 kg/m² of dead load, which most modern conservatory frames can accommodate without modification.
Ridge Beam
The ridge beam in a conservatory is the structural element at the apex of the roof that supports the ridge end of the roof rafters. In traditional polycarbonate conservatory construction, the ridge beam is typically aluminium extrusion. When converting to a solid roof, the ridge beam must be capable of carrying the revised loading from the new roof structure. In some conversions, the existing aluminium ridge beam is replaced or reinforced with a steel or timber beam sized to carry the solid roof loads.
Polycarbonate Roof
Polycarbonate conservatory roofs are formed from twin-wall or multi-wall extruded polycarbonate sheets, glazed into the aluminium or UPVC frame. Polycarbonate is lightweight and inexpensive but has significant shortcomings: it provides poor thermal insulation (typical U-value 1.8–3.5 W/m²K), is noisy in rain, discolours and becomes brittle with age, and creates extreme temperature swings (too hot in summer, too cold in winter). Replacing a polycarbonate roof with an insulated Metrotile solid roof transforms the comfort and usability of the conservatory. Polycarbonate sheets are removed panel-by-panel and the existing frame rebated to accept the new structure.

Contrast: Solid roof conversion

Glass Roof
Glass conservatory roofs use toughened or laminated safety glass in aluminium or UPVC frames. Glass provides better thermal performance than polycarbonate (typical double-glazed unit U-value 1.0–1.4 W/m²K) and does not discolour, but is heavy, expensive to replace and still creates overheating in summer and heat loss in winter relative to a fully insulated solid roof. Glass roofs can also be replaced with Metrotile solid roof systems, though the existing frame must be checked to ensure it can carry the revised structural loads.
Structural Load (Conservatory)
Structural load assessment for a conservatory solid roof conversion involves calculating the dead load (self-weight) of the new roof system, the imposed load (snow and maintenance access) and the wind load (uplift and lateral), and comparing these with the capacity of the existing conservatory frame, dwarf walls and foundations. Metrotile steel tiles at 7–8 kg/m² are the lightest tiled roofing option available, minimising the additional structural demand on the existing conservatory structure. Metrotile's technical team can provide load schedules for structural engineer review.

See also: Dead load · Snow load

Solid Roof Conversion

Solid Roof Conversion
Solid roof conversion is the process of replacing the polycarbonate or glass panels of an existing conservatory roof with an insulated solid roof structure, typically finished with roofing tiles or a flat roof membrane. In a Metrotile solid roof conversion, the existing conservatory frame is retained, a new timber framework is erected over the existing structure to form the rafters and battens, a breathable underlay is installed, and Metrotile lightweight steel tiles are fixed to battens. Insulation (typically rigid PIR foam boards) is installed between the rafters, and a plasterboard ceiling is fitted below. The result is a weathertight, insulated room suitable for year-round use.

Synonyms: Conservatory roof replacement · Roof conversion · Tiled conservatory roof

Insulated Solid Roof
An insulated solid roof is a composite roof assembly that combines structural elements (rafters, battens) with thermal insulation (PIR rigid foam boards or mineral wool) and an internal finish (plasterboard ceiling) to create a thermally efficient roof envelope. In a Metrotile conservatory conversion, the typical U-value achieved is 0.15–0.18 W/m²K for the roof element — meeting or exceeding current Part L Building Regulations requirements. This compares with a typical polycarbonate conservatory roof U-value of 1.8–3.5 W/m²K.

See also: Thermal upgrade · U-value

Roof Conversion System
A roof conversion system is a complete proprietary package comprising all the components needed to convert a polycarbonate or glass conservatory roof to a solid tiled roof: the structural framework, insulation, underlay, battens, tiles and accessories. Metrotile offers a complete solid roof conversion system with all components engineered and tested to work together, backed by the 40-year tile guarantee and BBA certification. Using a proprietary system simplifies specification, installation and warranty management.
Thermal Upgrade
Thermal upgrade in conservatory roofing refers to the improvement in thermal insulation performance achieved by replacing an uninsulated polycarbonate or glass roof with an insulated solid roof. A typical Metrotile solid roof conversion reduces heat loss through the roof by 90–95% compared to a polycarbonate roof (from U-value ~2.5 to ~0.16 W/m²K), dramatically reducing heating costs in winter and preventing overheating in summer. Thermal upgrade is the primary driver for most conservatory solid roof conversions.
Habitable Room
A habitable room is a room used for living, sleeping, studying or similar purposes — as defined in Building Regulations Approved Document F and Approved Document L. Under building regulations, a conservatory is specifically exempt from Part L (thermal) requirements, meaning it can have glazed walls and roof without meeting insulation standards. However, once a conservatory's roof is replaced with an insulated solid roof (and the separating wall between house and conservatory has adequate thermal performance), the space may be treated as a habitable room and used year-round — but Building Regulations approval may then be required.

See also: Part L compliance · Building control approval

Year-Round Use
Year-round use describes the goal of most conservatory solid roof conversions: to transform a seasonal space (too hot in summer, too cold in winter) into a comfortable room usable in all weather conditions. Year-round use requires adequate thermal insulation (solid roof and walls), adequate heating, adequate ventilation, and — if the room is to be habitable — compliance with Building Regulations. Metrotile solid roof systems, with their high insulation values and robust weatherproofing, are designed specifically to enable year-round use of conservatory and garden room spaces.

Regulations & Compliance

Part L Compliance (Conservatory)
Part L of the Building Regulations (Conservation of Fuel and Power) sets minimum thermal insulation standards for new and altered buildings. Conservatories are specifically exempt from Part L requirements — they do not need to meet minimum U-values — provided they are separated from the house by a thermally compliant wall and door, and are not heated by the house's fixed heating system. However, if a conservatory roof is replaced with a solid insulated roof in a way that makes the conservatory a permanent extension (habitually heated room), Part L compliance for the roof element (U-value ≤ 0.18 W/m²K) is required.

See also: Building Regulations · Habitable room

Part B — Fire Safety (Conservatory)
Part B of the Building Regulations (Fire Safety) requires that a habitable room at first-floor level or above has adequate means of escape in case of fire. If a conservatory conversion creates a first-floor habitable room (e.g., in a two-storey extension) with a solid roof, Part B requirements for window egress, protected escape routes or fire detection must be met. For single-storey conservatory conversions this is usually straightforward, but multi-storey configurations may require a structural engineer and building control assessment.
Planning Permission (Conservatory)
Planning permission is formal consent from the local planning authority (LPA) to carry out development. Most conservatory solid roof conversions do not require planning permission as they fall within permitted development rights — the conversion is considered maintenance rather than new development. However, planning permission is required if the property is listed, in a conservation area, or if the new roof changes the overall appearance of the structure beyond what permitted development allows. Always confirm with the LPA or a planning consultant before commencing work.

See also: Permitted development · Planning permission (general)

Permitted Development (Conservatory)
Permitted development rights allow certain types of building work to be carried out without requiring planning permission, subject to size and location limits. For conservatories and single-storey extensions, permitted development rights (under GDPO 2015, Schedule 2, Part 1, Class B) allow extensions up to specified size limits without planning permission. Replacing the roof of an existing permitted development conservatory with a solid roof is generally considered permitted development. Permitted development rights do not apply to properties where a permitted development Article 4 Direction has been made (common in conservation areas and some housing estates).
Building Control Approval (Conservatory)
Building control approval is required when a conservatory solid roof conversion makes the space a habitable room — triggering compliance with Part L (thermal), Part B (fire), Part F (ventilation) and Part P (electrical) of the Building Regulations. A building control officer will inspect the works and issue a completion certificate on satisfactory completion. In practice, many conservatory solid roof conversions are carried out without building control notification because the works are not considered to create a habitable room, but this is a grey area and professional advice is recommended.
Conservation Area (Conservatory)
Conservation areas are designated zones where the local planning authority seeks to preserve or enhance the character and appearance of the built environment. Properties in conservation areas have more restrictive permitted development rights: cladding the exterior of a house with non-traditional materials — including replacing a conservatory roof with a solid tiled roof — may require planning permission. Permitted development rights for extensions in conservation areas are also reduced. Check with the LPA before undertaking any external works on a conservatory in a conservation area.

See also: Conservation area (general)

Thermal & Acoustic Performance

U-Value (Conservatory Roof)
U-value measures the rate of heat transfer through a building element (W/m²K) — the lower the U-value, the better the insulation. A standard polycarbonate conservatory roof has a U-value of approximately 1.8–3.5 W/m²K. A Metrotile insulated solid roof conversion typically achieves a U-value of 0.15–0.18 W/m²K — a tenfold improvement. This transforms the heat loss characteristics of the roof and, in combination with improved heating, makes the conservatory a comfortable year-round room. Part L requires new and replacement roofs in habitable extensions to achieve U-value ≤ 0.18 W/m²K.

See also: U-value (general)

Condensation (Conservatory)
Condensation is a major problem in polycarbonate and glass conservatories — warm moist air from the house meets the cold glazed surfaces and moisture condenses. Solid roof conversions with adequate insulation eliminate this problem on the roof surface. However, interstitial condensation (condensation within the roof build-up) must be managed by correct positioning of the vapour control layer and adequate ventilation of the batten zone. A breathable membrane directly under the tiles and a VCL (vapour control layer) below the insulation prevents moisture migration.

See also: Breathable underlay · Dew point

Acoustic Improvement (Conservatory)
Acoustic improvement is one of the most immediately noticed benefits of a conservatory solid roof conversion. Polycarbonate and glass roofs amplify rain noise dramatically — a heavy shower can make conversation impossible. Replacing the glazed roof with an insulated solid roof clad in Metrotile stone chip tiles reduces rain noise by 20–30 dB (a reduction of approximately 75–94% in perceived loudness). The stone chip surface of Metrotile tiles provides better rain impact absorption than smooth metal roofing sheets. This acoustic improvement is consistently cited by homeowners as a key benefit of solid roof conversion.
